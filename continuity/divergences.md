Entry format: **Status** · **Canon** (what the original does, in our own words) · **Ours** · **Why** (if it isn't obvious) · **Home** (where it's written out in full) · **Ripples** (affected files, open questions).

**Retired, never reuse:** DIV-033, DIV-034, DIV-036, DIV-042 — the trio split across three Houses and its knock-ons (whole-year classes, Sirius's second break-in, the Boggart lesson House by House), reverted 2026-10-27. ### DIV-009 · The first meeting at Madam Malkin's

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco brags about brooms and houses, asks about Harry's family and sneers at Hagrid. Harry gets out before giving his surname, disliking him.
- **Ours:** sunlight on her hair reminds Harry of Disney's *Sleeping Beauty*, watched once in secret from the living-room doorway, the one time Aunt Petunia forgot to lock the cupboard. She opens an eye and asks whether he's for Hogwarts too, and he blurts out whether she's Sleeping Beauty. He goes up on tiptoe to check, finds her eyes aren't the princess's purple, and tells her hers are pretty too. She has had a great many compliments and never one this sincere or this awestruck, and she wants to keep it.
  - **The encounter starts well, and nothing in the first half is a feint.** She is charmed, he is disarmed, and the reader watches something good happen before anything spoils it.
  - **Then it has to be laundered.** The tale turns out to be Muggle, so the compliment becomes a thing she is not allowed to own. She doesn't lash out: she goes cold and starts checking. Who taught him Muggle stories, what happened to his parents, were they *our* kind. The instant he says both of them were magic she warms straight back up, delighted, and offers to bring him up to speed. **The snobbery is the price of keeping the compliment** — if it comes from a real wizard she may have it. The reasoning runs backwards, and it is exactly her.
  - **Hagrid is incidental, which is what makes it worse.** He turns up at the window, she repeats her father's opinion of him almost absently, and never notices the boy beside her go cold. There is no moment in the scene where she knows she has lost him.
  - **No names, and not by accident.** She asks for his name last, and the shop's clerk has him off the stool and away to the counter before he can answer (DIV-115).
  - **She goes home delighted:** *Mama! I made a new friend!* Her parents hear about him that evening, which is what makes the train a wound rather than a snub (DIV-010, [hogwarts-express](../scenes/b1/hogwarts-express.md)). What it does to her father: [lucius-malfoy](../characters/lucius-malfoy.md).
  - **Harry leaves thinking the princess is cruel,** and regrets being nice.
- **Why:** the souring has to leave her happy, or she can't go home and say it. A girl who knows she's been caught is lashing out; this one is simply being herself in front of someone who has stopped listening. It also puts the Muggle books' motive on the page (DIV-010), and shows the engine that runs her for seven books: when a thing she wants turns out not to be allowed, she finds a way to be allowed it rather than giving it up.
- **Home:** [scenes/b1/madam-malkins.md](../scenes/b1/madam-malkins.md)
- **Ripples:** DIV-010, DIV-040, DIV-115 · Q-150 · [threads/sleeping-beauty.md](../threads/sleeping-beauty.md) · [threads/harry-dracaena.md](../threads/harry-dracaena.md) · [characters/lucius-malfoy.md](../characters/lucius-malfoy.md)

### DIV-010 · Dracaena's Muggle books

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco shows no interest in anything Muggle.
- **Ours:** she wants to know what the boy saw, and asks for the books outright that same evening, in front of both parents. Narcissa sends Dobby for them (Q-018, answered): many versions of Sleeping Beauty, other fairy tales, even a picture book of the Disney film. She reads them before term starts and begins casting herself and the boy as a fairy tale. Fairy tales become her gateway to Muggle novels (wizards are a small minority and don't write many) and eventually to Muggle Studies (DIV-012). She tuts at the Muggles who get real magic wrong.
  - **Nothing is hidden, and she isn't sneaking anywhere.** It is far too early in her life for that, and she has no reason to: she has just established to her own satisfaction that the boy was a proper wizard, which is the thing that made the story keepable at all (DIV-009).
  - **Lucius acquiesces because Narcissa wants to indulge her daughter**, not because he is persuaded of anything. That is the entire transaction, and it is the house in miniature (DIV-030).
  - **Which puts the whole Muggle line on his ledger from the start.** Muggle Studies in B3 is then an escalation of something long permitted rather than a rebellion (DIV-012, Q-020).
  - **And it is Dobby** — which is a footnote next to everything else he does for that house. When Harry frees him in B2 the books are the least of what the Malfoys lose (DIV-076).
- **Why:** a gradual way out of her bigotry. And it's cute.

### DIV-011 · Dracaena is gifted and brave

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco is a capable student who leans on his father's name and folds under real danger.
- **Ours:** An excellent potioneer, cunning, and a voracious reader on whatever grips her: poisons, the strange boy's stories. Competent and no coward, as befits wizard royalty.
  - **Potions:** the best potioneer in her year, and possibly in the castle, thanks to a six-year head start with Snape as her master (DIV-031). Hermione comes second. At least in Potions, she isn't the brightest witch of her generation.
  - **B1, the first Potions lesson:** Snape has her answer the questions Harry can't, to show her off. Hermione blurts out the answer to the third and loses points: right by the book, imprecise in practice. "Anything to add, Miss Malfoy?" Dracaena corrects her from years of experience and wins points for Slytherin. Hermione's hatred of her starts here.
  - **B6, the first Potions lesson:** Harry beats her at the Draught of Living Death using the Prince's notes. The sixth-year Prince's tricks are experimental and less elegant than the method the adult Snape taught her, and she has no drive left to one-up Harry: her priorities are elsewhere (DIV-015). She and Hermione both notice how much his new tricks resemble her technique. In the same lesson she knows the Amortentia and keeps her hand down (DIV-045).
- **Why:** her skill needs a source, and Hermione needs taking down a peg.

### DIV-012 · Muggle Studies: Burbage's best student

- **Status:** settled. Hermione dropping it is draft.
- **Canon:** Draco never takes Muggle Studies. Hermione takes it in B3 and drops it.
- **Ours:** Dracaena takes Muggle Studies and is Charity Burbage's best student. *(draft)* Hermione takes it in B3 and drops it, as in canon: she grew up among Muggles, and what's left is mostly wizards getting Muggles wrong. A timetable clash gives her the excuse. Dracaena frames it as Hermione conceding defeat.
  - **The interest is cold.** Grudging respect for a worthy foe, not affection: Muggles have goods worth taking — the fairy tales first, then novels and their inventiveness in general — and she takes them. She never warms to Muggles themselves.
  - **Burbage is a door, not a mentor.** "Best student" means she's invested in part of the curriculum and isn't an idiot. There's no bond, and no warmth to betray in B7 (DIV-016).
- **Why:** the Muggle-born dropping the subject while the pure-blood heiress keeps it is a sharper irony than a shared rivalry, and it makes Dracaena Burbage's only star pupil for DIV-016.

### DIV-013 · B4–B5: the dream

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco's hostility toward Harry never lets up. In B5 he joins Umbridge's Inquisitorial Squad.
- **Ours:** Harry's feats (Quirrell, the Basilisk, the Dementors) repair her opinion of him, because she prizes competence, daring and valour.
  - **What the script actually is** *(settled)*. Living outside it is **not** a wish to stop being a Malfoy: she loves the line, the nobility and being the latest of an unbroken descent of great people. The question underneath is what she is worth when none of that is counted — if she is defined by what she inherited, what remains when it is stripped away. What she wants is **one beautiful thing to call her own**, and the first one that ever turns up is an unassuming boy in a tailor's shop (DIV-009). It is why her B3 Boggart is him ([remedial-boggart](../scenes/b3/remedial-boggart.md), DIV-017). So she accepts Harry's invitation to the Yule Ball, half-believing he might really be her prince. Being his Second Task hostage tips her fully into the dream. She keeps her edge, but for the rest of B4 and nearly all of B5 she's simply happy with him. Her cover is "keeping tabs on Potter", a strategy Lucius himself comes up with at the end of B4, when she refuses his order to cut ties with Potter (DIV-040), with the Inquisitorial Squad on top.
  - **The cover is for her father and the Dark Lord, and for nobody else** *(settled)*. **There is no secret girlfriend.** They are public essentially from the Yule Ball and unmistakably so once she claims him at the lake (DIV-038), and the school has known since September anyway ([the-ferret](../scenes/b4/the-ferret.md), DIV-091). What Lucius reports upwards needs her close to Potter, which is exactly what everyone can see.
  - **So the Inquisitorial Squad does not square with it, and does not have to, because she does not care.** The badge and the prefectship are toys (Q-070). She is chummy with Harry in front of Umbridge, they quarrel constantly, and she takes points off him for absurd invented infractions — which is their game being played in public rather than a performance for anybody's benefit.
  - **Her account of herself, if anyone asks:** *I'm disciplining him. I'll make a proper wizard out of Potter yet, and someone has to.* A mercy and an obligation, delivered perfectly candidly, which is her backhanded-compliment register exactly.
  - **It vexes Umbridge and nobody can do anything about it**, because the school knows better and she is so entirely herself that there is nothing to take hold of. **It is also the clearest demonstration in the series that she believes she is above any rule at all** — which is her father's work, and which B6 breaks (DIV-030, DIV-015).

### DIV-015 · Between B5 and B6: the death of faith

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco takes the Dark Mark and the mission to kill Dumbledore, as punishment for Lucius's failure. He's proud of it until it crushes him.
- **Ours:** She is Marked and co-opted (canon default), but she's never proud of it: she's broken from the outset, ashamed of the Mark and defiled by it, a constant reminder of the chains that hold her. That summer she meets Voldemort for the first time, and her Occlumency (DIV-020) is no match for him: he shatters her walls with ease. He doesn't care that she "lied". He wants obedience and people broken into submission, and her love for Potter is only more suffering to inflict. She realises her prince can't possibly win, because Voldemort is too powerful. The dream was never real. She sinks into despair and panic and goes along with the assassination plot, to at least save her parents. She's back in the cage, and the promise she just made is broken. Back at school she doesn't end it at once: for a few weeks she's distant and hollow, only physical with Harry, and she quits the Quidditch team. When Flitwick reopens the Duelling Club, Harry offers it as a date. She refuses, and their fight ends in the breakup, brutal enough to lead in the end to Sectumsempra. B6 is the nightmare.

### DIV-017 · B3: her play-acting, and what it costs

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco milks his hippogriff injury for weeks. Slytherin uses it to postpone their match against Gryffindor, and he makes Harry and Ron prepare his Potions ingredients. He also joins the fake-Dementor prank at the Ravenclaw match.
- **Ours:**
  - **The injury:** she milks it for weeks too. She's not above subterfuge and pettiness when it comes to throwing darts at her target.
  - **The match:** she uses the injury to delay the match against Harry, because after losing in B2 she knows she needs more practice.
  - **Potions:** she badgers Harry into preparing her ingredients and turns him into her servant. When his poor technique spoils the potion she steps in, strangely sweet under the barbs. In her head they're a married couple cooking together, until she realises she's daydreaming in front of the whole class and scrambles to save face. Harry is weirded out.
  - **The lesson:** later she realises her play-acting gave Lucius the fuel to have Buckbeak executed. That lesson in consequences is what pushes her to save him.
  - **The prank:** she takes no part in the fake-Dementor prank at the Ravenclaw match. Crabbe and Goyle pull it on their own initiative, thinking she'll approve, and are confused when she greatly disapproves. **She had been doing the impression herself** that autumn, after the swarm dropped him off his broom, and then she faced her own Boggart and stopped. Not pity: **taste** (DIV-070). Having been unable to move, she now knows exactly what the joke is, and it is squalid. She never explains the new rule to anyone, including herself, and Harry notices only an absence ([lap-pillow](../scenes/b3/lap-pillow.md), Q-035).
  - **The Boggart:** with her wand arm in a sling, she sits out her own class's Boggart lesson. Harry never sees it: canon's lesson is Gryffindor's, and Slytherin isn't in the room. Lupin later goads her into a remedial lesson: she'd have faced a Boggart when Harry never has. **It is Harry, and he is indifferent**, telling her he wishes he'd never met her. She doesn't freeze, she comes apart: on her knees, crying, begging him not to go, and when she reaches for him the Boggart raises a wand at her without its face changing — disposal, not anger. Not a hateful Harry — hate is a reaction, and a reaction would mean she registered. This is being discarded as a non-entity, which is the only thing in the world that frightens her. Lupin deals with it and hands her a true, incomplete cover story. She understands none of it, and tells nobody, in B3 or after. When she does tell Harry at the lap pillow that she *froze*, she is lying ([remedial-boggart](../scenes/b3/remedial-boggart.md)).
  - **Why it's him.** Everything she has was handed to her; Harry is the first unexpected thing that ever happened to her, and the first person to say something to her that wasn't a nicety (DIV-009). She doesn't want to stop being a Malfoy — she loves the line and the nobility. The question underneath is what she is worth when none of it is counted, and what she chases is one beautiful thing to call her own. So the Boggart isn't *he doesn't love me*, it's **the script closing over the one gap she ever found in it**, which is also DIV-015 in rehearsal.
  - **The exam.** She is not over it. In June she fails the Boggart in Lupin's Defence exam, in front of her whole year, and this time there are witnesses ([the-trunk](../scenes/b3/the-trunk.md), DIV-093, DIV-094).

### DIV-018 · B3 climax: the swarm, the confession, the flight to Sirius

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** The Dementors converge on the lake, drawn by Black. Harry is saved by a Patronus his time-travelling self casts. Then he and Hermione fly Buckbeak to free Sirius.
- **Ours:**
  - **The lead:** to make room to free Buckbeak, she plants a fake lead with the Ministry that draws the Dementors to the lake (how: Q-025).
  - **The lake:** she can't control Buckbeak and ends up at the lake herself. She knows the Patronus Charm, having watched Harry attempt it and studied it for him, but she tries and can't cast it (DIV-027).
  - **The Patronus:** Harry re-evaluates her. She stole Buckbeak to save him, the lap pillow and her Patronus study weren't jokes, and now she's trying to drive the Dementors off herself: there's something more to this girl. His Patronus drives off the swarm and saves everyone, powered by the memory of the lap-pillow scene. As he casts there's a first flicker of resonance between them (DIV-021): love, however vague, is already there. How it works is open (Q-028).
  - **The confession:** under the Dementors' influence she breaks down sobbing and confesses, unprompted, that the swarm is her fault.
  - **What the swarm shows her:** her Boggart, because it is the worst memory she owns — a fake Harry, met in a classroom two terms earlier, telling her he wishes he'd never met her (DIV-017). She begs it not to leave her, not to ignore her, aloud, at nothing. **She never says his name**, and Harry, fighting a hundred Dementors, has no attention to spare for it: the reader gets everything, he gets noise. It lands just before he casts, so the memory she gave him drives off the thing making her relive losing him.
  - **Hermione's idea:** ask her to fly Buckbeak to Sirius.
  - **Why she agrees:** she owes Harry, and knows it. And she's just been thrust into the fairy tale: her prince drove off a horde of demons, for her.
  - **Sirius:** he recognises Narcissa's daughter, and they talk briefly. Maybe the Black family made something good after all, if Narcissa gave birth to a girl this brave.
- **Note:** This builds on 🔒 DIV-003 without changing it. The canon sequence has to be rebuilt without Time-Turners: Q-003.

### DIV-020 · Occlumency: the Malfoys, and Snape's lessons

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco is an Occlumens, taught by his aunt Bellatrix (mentioned in B6). Lucius isn't shown to be one.
- **Ours:**
  - **The family:** no one is born an Occlumens, but the discipline the Malfoys instil lends itself to learning it.
  - **Lucius** is a skilled Occlumens. He can't lie to Voldemort's face as Snape does, but in their regular B5 meetings he tweaks what Voldemort perceives through passive Legilimency. He tells Voldemort he has planted his daughter as a spy on Potter (DIV-013). Voldemort never comes to the Manor before B6.
  - **Dracaena** learns Occlumency from Snape in the summer between B4 and B5 (the author has the hook). Snape's cover holds: he's a fine Occlumens, and he has plausible deniability with Lucius, since he trained her as a general precaution for someone working under cover, not to deceive Voldemort.
  - **It isn't enough.** In summer 1996 Voldemort shatters her walls with ease (DIV-015). In the final duel she uses it another way (DIV-021).
- **Why:** Legilimency isn't mind reading, and Voldemort is very arrogant. That leaves Lucius room to tweak and Snape room to hide.

### DIV-041 · The rival in her head is Hermione

- **Status:** settled. **Amended 2026-11-17: the imagined rival was Cho and is now Hermione Granger.** Every Cho reading is purged from the bible; the Cho half of the entry survives only as what we do *not* do. The reasoning for the old version is in the git history.
- **Canon:** Harry has a crush on Cho Chang from B3, asks her to the Ball, finds she is going with Cedric, and takes Parvati. Hermione is his friend, plainly and uncomplicatedly, and nobody mistakes it for anything else.
- **Ours:** Harry never asks Cho, Dracaena is the only person he asks (🔒 DIV-004, DIV-019), and **there is no triangle anywhere in the series.** What there is is a jealousy that exists in exactly one head, and it is aimed at Hermione.
  - **Cho is not a rival and never was.** We plant nothing between her and Harry at any point, so leaving her standing as the imagined rival was promising the reader a thread we have no intention of paying. Dracaena's entire opinion of her is that she is *a third-rate Seeker* — which, having beaten her, she regards as settled fact rather than spite. **Even she can see there is nothing there.**
  - **Which does not save Cho in B5** *(settled, Q-134)*. Canon's beats are kept almost whole — she seeks Harry out on the train, in the Owlery, and so on — and **Dracaena is lurking not far behind every single time.** Covert in company, outright terrifying when the two of them are alone. **Mostly played for gags**, and the entire school clocks what is going on; Harry is the only person in the castle who does not.
  - **Harry's side is mild annoyance.** He wishes Dracaena would turn up and get him out of it — the same register as Fleur's teasing, where he can tell there is a war on around him and does not much mind ([the-little-basilisk](../scenes/b4/the-little-basilisk.md), DIV-038).
  - **It is explicitly not defence and must never be played as jealousy.** She knows perfectly well she has won. An affront was offered, and her father's rule is that every affront is paid back so that nobody tries again ([dracaena-malfoy](../characters/dracaena-malfoy.md)). Forgiveness is close to alien to her, and this is what that looks like aimed at somebody the reader has no stake in.
  - **Hermione is always there, and that is the whole mechanism.** She feeds him, makes him do his work beside her so she can catch his mistakes, has his broom confiscated for his own good, and slaps people on his behalf. From the outside, a girl who does all of that has a claim on him — and Dracaena has been watching her do it since B1.
  - **Hermione means none of it, and never has.** Harry is a scrawny, miserable boy she appointed herself to fix; she sees him as a brother and nothing else, and it is suffocating rather than romantic ([hermione-granger](../characters/hermione-granger.md)). **The misreading is total, one-way and never corrected on the page.**
  - **Canon's B4 does the work for us.** Ron is not speaking to Harry for weeks after the Goblet, so Harry and Hermione spend the whole autumn visibly alone together, and Dracaena watches a month of it without anybody having to arrange a scene.
  - **It starts at the World Cup.** The veela leave him cold (DIV-037), so she knows he loves somebody; Hermione is sitting with him; she picks the obvious answer and is wrong by one person.
  - **It ends twice.** Hermione walks into the Yule Ball on Viktor Krum's arm — the rival removed in public at the exact moment Dracaena is on Harry's — and then Fleur's line settles it outright, and she understands that the person he loves is her ([the-little-basilisk](../scenes/b4/the-little-basilisk.md), DIV-038). *(Available and not forced: the boy who took her imagined rival off the board is the one Harry puts down in the maze.)*

### DIV-050 · B7: Dobby dies of exhaustion

- **Status:** settled. What elves are: DIV-075.
- **Canon:** Dobby Apparates everyone out of Malfoy Manor and dies on arrival at Shell Cottage, hit by Bellatrix's thrown knife.
- **Ours:** He spends himself ferrying people out on his own Apparition, though he warned them he can't carry many: first the prisoners, then the Malfoys, his former masters, because Harry asked him to save "his family". Harry, last out, holds off Bellatrix and the others. Then Dobby has nothing left, and crumbles into dust.
  - **He has been dying since B2.** Unhoused, he reverts by degrees towards the true shape of an elf, and the true shape needs a house to sustain, and he has not got one. The reversion *is* the dying, spread over five years and spent on Harry's errands. Harry asking him in B3 whether he has got taller is the first sign, and nobody — Dobby included — reads it as anything but freedom agreeing with him (DIV-075).
  - **Feeding him buys nothing.** Unhoused, he has to eat for the first time in his existence, and does, very well, out of Harry's vault (Q-102). Food holds the body up and nothing holds the spirit up, so he is the best-fed creature in Britain and still runs out on schedule. The early years of it make him feel better than he ever has, which is exactly why nobody is alarmed.
  - **Kreacher knew.** Alone of everyone, he could see it, and said so repeatedly as an insult, and was ignored (DIV-078).
  - **Why the Manor, and why repeatedly.** The residual claim still holds: he is still partly the Manor's spirit, which is the only reason his Apparition works there at all and works more than once. He spends his house to empty it, and the trip that takes him out for the last time is the one with no return.
  - **The true form, in public.** As the body crumbles, what has been surfacing for five years finally stands: a translucent figure taller than a man, fair-haired, with Malfoy grey eyes — the house wearing the family's likeness, because that is what it was made of. **Everyone present sees it, and nobody knows what it means.** Lucius recognises *a Malfoy* and has no context whatever for why. Nothing is explained in the moment.
  - **His tomb comes from Winky.** Three years earlier Dobby asked Harry to give her a grave, because he thought she should be honoured and had no idea he was describing his own death. Nobody asks this time; Harry simply knows to, and the knowing is Dobby's own doing (DIV-077).
  - **Hermione explains it afterwards.** She is the only person alive who can read the image, because she spent B5 in the archives (DIV-075). Telling the Malfoys what they were looking at is the payoff of her research and the only moment her work ever changes anything for anyone.
  - **His death is real** — not a body discarded and remade, as Kreacher's would be — precisely because he was unhoused. There is nothing left to make him again. Freedom is what makes it final, and he chooses it anyway.
- **Why:** his death becomes a choice made out of love, not a stray knife. The true form collapses the two halves of pureblood ideology into one image in front of the man who holds both: the thing he kicked wears his own blood's face.

### DIV-066 · The Snitch carries the score

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** The Snitch is worth 150 points and ends the match. One catch decides almost every game, so the Seeker is the only player who matters and the other six play for pride. The Quidditch Cup goes on points scored across the season, and Quidditch feeds the House Cup.
- **Ours:** The Snitch is worth 30, still ends the match, and **carries the score**: every point either side puts up weighs it down, so it begins the match untouchable and ends it catchable. The Seeker stops waiting on luck and starts deciding when the match ends, with an opponent trying to decide otherwise.
  - **The burden is the combined score,** which makes every goal double-edged: scoring is how you come back, and scoring is what arms the other Seeker to end the match on you.
  - **Nobody can sit on a lead,** because only goals bring the Snitch into reach.
  - **The closing move.** Both Seekers want it when the margin is under 30; only the leader wants it when the margin is over. Catching it from behind is a concession, to stop the bleeding and save the aggregate.
  - **What a Seeker does:** no bat, and no touching the Quaffle. Disruption is bluff, not force, and leaving your opposite number costs you the seconds she's unmarked, so the two of them glue themselves together without any rule saying so.
  - **The numbers.** Goals stay at 10 and ordinary match totals stay modest, so 30 is three goals: a real swing, never a game in itself. A decider runs longer and higher, because the Cup margin forces the leader to keep attacking: the B3 final ends 140–50 (DIV-069).
  - **The Quidditch Cup** goes on aggregate, as in canon. **The House Cup** takes a flat prize per match won, never the match score, so the change doesn't touch it.
  - **The deadlock.** A scoreless match never weighs the Snitch down and never ends, which is where the legendary months-long games came from.
- **Why:** at 150 the Seeker is a lottery ticket. At 30 the job is a decision with an opponent attached, which is what the rivalry needs, and the other six players finally have a reason to exist: Chasers run the clock, not just the score.

### DIV-067 · B5: the pitch kiss, the brawl and the hollow Cup

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Gryffindor beat Slytherin in the first match of the year. Draco taunts Harry about his dead mother; Harry and George attack him, Fred is held back by his teammates, and Umbridge bans all three for life. Gryffindor go on to win the Quidditch Cup without Harry, with Ron redeemed in the final and Ginny catching the Snitch in his place.
- **Ours:** Dracaena would never insult Harry's mother, so the fight starts somewhere else: with her being happy.
  - **The match.** Slytherin lead by less than 30 when Harry takes the Snitch off her and steals it. Under DIV-066 her one job with that lead was to stop him reaching it, and she didn't.
  - **Why she lost.** Not a thrown match. She was flying *with* him instead of against him, matching him when she should have been blocking, because by now the duel is worth more to her than the result. She has never beaten him and stopped expecting to years ago.
  - **The kiss.** She comes off the pitch elated, barely minding the loss and a little sorry it's over, and kisses him in front of a full stadium. The school has known since the Second Task (DIV-038); what it has never seen is the loser delighted that the winner won.
  - **The accusation.** Her house reads it as throwing the match. It is false, and she cannot deny it, because the only honest answer is that she was enjoying herself too much to stop him.
  - **Montague** says it where Harry can hear: she's gone blood-traitor, like the Weasleys, and worse. Naming them gives George his own reason to swing. Harry gets there first.
  - **The ban.** Fred is held back and banned anyway, as in canon, for the extra outrage. Umbridge bans all three for life.

### DIV-068 · Brooms amplify the rider

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Brooms are a ladder. A better broom is faster and better in every way, the Slytherin team's Nimbus 2001s are bought advantage, and a Firebolt outclasses everything on the pitch.
- **Ours:** Power and obedience come together, so a broom transmits its rider rather than carrying them. Cheap brooms are forgiving, stable and slow; top brooms are responsive to the point of wildness and do exactly what they're asked, including what the rider didn't mean to ask. Money buys amplification, not performance, and the gap between two top brooms shows only at the very edge of what either can do.
  - **Temperament decides fit.** A wild broom suits Harry, who flies the way he duels. It's the wrong instrument for Dracaena, who is precise and controlled, so a Firebolt would make her slower (DIV-069).
  - **Assistance, not just obedience.** The two top lines differ in kind, not rank. The Nimbus line's flying charm is *opinionated*: it manages the small corrections — angling into a turn, holding a line — so the rider can spend attention on scouting and duelling, and it quietly declines an instruction it reads as a mistake. A Firebolt does nothing for you and refuses nothing. It obeys, including stray thoughts, so riding one is a constant tax on attention, and the reward is that nothing is off the table.
  - **So the better broom costs more to fly.** Harry spends the whole B3 final minding his Firebolt and is nearly spent by the end; Dracaena never once fights hers. He wins on the one move where he stops minding it and simply trusts it (DIV-069).
  - **It explains canon.** Lucius buys seven top brooms and his team loses on older ones anyway, Dracaena included in B2.
- **Why:** without it, Quidditch is decided by whose father spends the most, which is a poor foundation for a rivalry and worse for the B3 final, where she has to lose fairly. This fixes it without regulating equipment, so canon's Firebolt subplot survives untouched.

### DIV-069 · B3: the Quidditch final, and the hand

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco jeers at Hagrid over Buckbeak's sentence and Hermione slaps him. Days later Slytherin foul their way through the final, Gryffindor pile up points regardless, Harry outflies Draco to the Snitch, and Gryffindor take the Cup.
- **Ours:** the same two scenes, turned to show Harry who she is, and to teach her that playing straight gets her what she wants.
  - **The jeer.** She still says it, to keep up appearances with her house, and she's almost in tears as she does. She baits Hermione deliberately and doesn't flinch when the slap lands: she thinks she has it coming. Hermione sees only the jeer; **Harry sees her face**, and isn't sure what he saw. A faint alarm bell.
  - **Flint's strategy.** Slytherin foul their way through the final as in canon, and here it is a plan rather than spite. Flint knows his Chasers are the weaker side, so he stops playing for the match and plays for the aggregate: batter the Gryffindor Chasers, hold the margin under the Cup line, and have his Seeker end the game early. It costs him the match and would have won him the Cup.
  - **The clean player.** She is the only Slytherin who doesn't foul, and Harry gets two different versions of it. In private, mid-air, she tells him she doesn't want to win like this and that some of them have dignity — sincerity only he hears, and he has no reason to credit it. In public she finally snaps and flies at her own Beater, and what the stadium sees is not principle but a tantrum about her match being taken from her. He has both halves and can't reconcile them, which is the point (Q-035).
  - **Two moments neither of them takes.** At the most catchable the Snitch has been all match, she leaves her post to scream at her Beater and he leaves his to check on his Chaser. Neither notices. It is the first time either has chosen something over the Snitch, and it is the behaviour she gets accused of in B5 (DIV-067).
  - **The margin, not the lead.** Gryffindor are ahead almost throughout and it never helps them: they need to be 60 clear before Harry may take the Snitch, or Slytherin keep the Cup on aggregate. Every Slytherin goal pushes that line back by a goal. Harry's job is therefore the same all match — stop her ending it — and the phases belong to her, not to him.
  - **Her phases.** For most of the match she hunts freely: catching the Snitch caps Gryffindor's margin and saves the Cup, which means losing the match on purpose, and she does it willingly and hates it. Once Gryffindor reach the margin her job inverts — she can no longer take her eyes off him and has to mark instead of hunt. Her rage is at being contained, not at being confused.
  - **The fake-out.** The margin comes within reach, Harry breaks for the Snitch, she goes with him — and her own Chasers score, which pushes the finish line back a goal and forces him to break off. She is saved by her team without their knowing it. He turns and gives her a smug look: *I could end this whenever I want.*
  - **The real duel** is over in a moment. She sticks far closer this time, having seen his acceleration, and it isn't enough.
  - **Why she loses.** Two things, and neither is equipment. **She is legible to him**, because she has watched him fly for two years and he has learned what the watching looks like from the inside: he baits the trick she is waiting for. And **she will not be reckless.** The winning move is an improvisation she finds distasteful, not one she lacks the skill for (DIV-070). At the lake she says so herself, unprompted: she would never have done it, it's uncouth.
  - **The cost is his.** He pays all match, minding a broom that obeys every stray thought, and is nearly spent by the end; she never fights hers (DIV-068). He wins on the one move where he stops minding it.
  - **Nothing to blame.** A better broom wouldn't have helped her, and the reader has watched the better broom cost Harry all afternoon. She never reaches for the excuse: what she names at the lake is taste, not equipment, which is the more damning answer because it is about who she is. Lucius doesn't offer her a Firebolt either, before or after — he celebrates achievement, he doesn't console failure.
  - **The gloves.** They come onto the pitch wearing each other's — she in a Seeker's glove she won off him at the Duelling Club, he in one of hers with her initial stitched into the palm (DIV-059). It is the first time the trophies are visible to anyone else, it is played as a joke by both, and neither mentions them again. He catches the Snitch with her glove on his hand.
  - **The hair tie.** It comes loose mid-duel and he takes it out of the air without deciding to — his broom giving him the reach because it obeys what he hasn't said (DIV-068) — and pockets it. At the lake he gives it back, and she turns round and starts to ask him to tie it for her before Madam Hooch arrives and cuts her off. Being interrupted is what lets her pretend tomorrow that she never asked.
  - **The crack opens.** The school already jokes about them; this is where her own house stops laughing. Mid-match she answers a jibe of his with a Muggle cheer — Muggle Studies showing in the worst possible place (DIV-012) — and the Slytherin stands go quiet and scowl. She calls her own Beater inbred in front of witnesses (DIV-070), abandons her post to do it, and ends the day taking Harry's hand on the pitch. It is written home about. B5's accusation lands on ground this prepares (Q-081).
  - **Alone on the pitch.** Her team desert her. She sits there staring at her hands and cries: she played it straight, spent everything she had, and lost anyway.
  - **The hand.** Harry, hauled off by his own side, looks back, sees her alone, and goes to her. He crouches and holds out a hand: he had a good time flying against her, and can they do it again? It mirrors the train, where she offered hers and he didn't take it (DIV-009). She wants to believe he's being snide and can't, and takes it.
  - **The race.** He's off his broom and on it again, challenging her to a race, and she chases him. They both get detention.
  - **The mask goes back on** the next morning, colder than ever, which confuses Harry badly. The same reflex as the Potions daydream (DIV-017).
- **Why:** it answers Q-035 with the two strongest supporting scenes in B3, and it gives her the second half of her Buckbeak motive: the guilt makes her want to act, and this makes her believe acting straight will work (DIV-003, [buckbeak-rescue](../scenes/b3/buckbeak-rescue.md)).

### DIV-070 · Dracaena's objections are aesthetic

- **Status:** draft
- **Canon:** Draco's prejudice is plain bigotry with no reasoning under it, and his snobbery is a character flaw and nothing more.
- **Ours:** her taste is her moral channel. Things are wrong to her because they are *ugly*, beneath her, unbecoming of what she thinks she is, and that single principle runs most of what she does.
  - **How it shows.** Slytherin's fouling disgusts her because it is squalid, not because it is unfair. Buckbeak's execution offends her because butchering a beast on a grudge does not befit nobility. Her insult of choice, under real anger, is *inbred* (B3 final), and *uncouth* is what she calls the move that beats her.
  - **It is a way out of the ideology, not a rejection of it.** She does not decide blood purity is wrong. She accepts the premise and notices her own class failing it: the lines have thinned, the specimens in front of her at her own table are not impressive, and she can see it (DIV-008, DIV-035). Crabbe and Goyle are her standing private evidence; she keeps them because they are useful and because it is expected.
  - **The same move as Muggle Studies.** Cold evaluation on merit rather than loyalty, applied to her own side instead of the other one (DIV-012). Two exits from the same door, both running on her snobbery rather than against it.
  - **It is also her ceiling.** She loses the B3 final because winning required an improvisation she finds distasteful, and she says so at the lake. Her taste is her compass and the thing that stops her: in B5 she loses again to something her code does not price (enjoying herself), and in B6 Harry grabs a spell he doesn't know — the same recklessness she called uncouth — and it nearly kills her (DIV-032, DIV-043).
- **Why:** it makes her bigotry arguable rather than inert, gives her a route out that doesn't require a conversion scene, and turns the one arena she can never win into a fact about her character instead of a fact about Harry's broom (DIV-069).

### DIV-072 · Painted life: the Dessendre gift

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Wizarding paintings all move and talk, and nobody distinguishes between kinds. Portraits are furniture and occasionally useful.
- **Ours:** canon's portraits are unchanged — echoes with manners, not people. Against them stands one exception: the Dessendres, an extinct French family whose ancestral gift made paintings that were actually alive. Theirs is the only real painted life there has ever been.
  - **Fenced by its own nature.** Ancestral and blood-bound, so there is nothing to learn; not spellcasting, so radicals don't touch it (DIV-007); extinct for about a century before B1, so there is nobody to ask. A canvas is also trivially destroyed from outside, which is why almost none of it survived. Nobody hides from a war in a painted world because the people who could make one are dead.
  - **The story.** The son died; the mother painted her family back and went in after them; the father went in after *her* and lost years to it; the younger daughter, Alicia, followed him in to help and hadn't the skill to get out again. The elder, Clea, never went in — she acted from outside to end it, failed, left them to it, and regretted it for twenty years. The end came from outside and had nothing to do with any of it — the manor burned, and the line ended there. How Verso died is never stated or asked. How it went for the people inside stays vague on purpose, under two constraints: no creature turning on its maker, and the fire is an ordinary accident rather than a verdict (Q-086).
  - **Being inside.** It wastes a living body by degrees, over years; the painted themselves are untouched by it. **You can leave whenever you like** — a canvas is not a trap, so Aline stayed because she wanted to and Renoir stayed because she wouldn't come. The exception is skill: getting out is the painter's craft, and Alicia hadn't got it. This makes the Dessendres a story about choosing rather than about being caught, which is the same story as B6.
  - **It lives in memory, not in a library.** The true version was never written down where anyone could find it; it is a story the old houses tell because the Dessendres were their sort. Hermione's books have the magic and the fire and no people at all. That is DIV-008's cleanest demonstration — what her class has is unbroken memory, not better scholarship — and it licenses the vagueness, because a told story has gaps nobody can check.
  - **The portraits despise it.** They will not discuss painted life, because a portrait that admits it exists is admitting what it is. It is why the one surviving canvas has hung unmentioned for a century, and why the ghosts, not the paintings, are who you ask.
  - **Renoir is Harry's parallel, and the more dangerous one.** Aline retreated into the dream, which is Dracaena. Renoir went in for the best reason there is, to bring someone he loved out, and the painting took him anyway. Harry daydreams too, but forward (DIV-043); Renoir is the warning that forward-facing and selfless is not enough on its own. B6 and B7 put Harry in his exact position, and Harry does what he could not: he doesn't follow her under, he gets her out (DIV-049, DIV-052).
  - **Dracaena has the true version** and takes the opposite lesson from Harry's: not a warning, a failed experiment. A technique that nearly worked, used badly. *Why not paintings?* is a question the girl who answers everything with a method has genuinely asked (DIV-011).
  - **One object.** Clea's landscape hangs unregarded in the fifth-floor corridor to the prefects' bathroom: creatures she painted as a girl, before any of the tragedy, still waiting for a mistress who grew up and stopped coming. The one who speaks is a headless clay figure grinding boulders in his own chest to finish a castle for her, and she never gave him a name. It survives only because it was out of the house when the manor burned. The staff don't know what it is, the portraits won't say, and the ghosts will if asked properly.
  - **No Dessendre ever appears on the page**, in any book. History and one painting, not a cast.
- **Why:** canon asserts the Mirror's lesson and never demonstrates it. This demonstrates it at the scale the series needs — not a boy who stared too long but a family who could actually build the dream and were destroyed anyway — and gives the escapism theme a case outside Dracaena's head that Harry carries from B1. It also feeds the decline (DIV-008, DIV-035): one more thing the wizarding world used to be able to do.

### DIV-073 · B1: the Dessendres, and the corridor

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Dumbledore tells Harry that men have wasted away before the Mirror, not knowing whether what it showed was real or possible. The warning is an assertion, and Harry takes it on trust. Nothing follows from it.
- **Ours:** he gives it a name — the Dessendres were nearly ruined by a magic like this one — and Harry goes and looks.
  - **Why he reaches for a hundred-year-old French family.** Not because it is famous; by B1 the public barely has the story (DIV-072). Because it is what *he* thinks about. He is half telling on himself, to a boy who has just seen his dead parents, and it reads differently on a reread after B7.
  - **Hermione delivers the public version**, with relish, and the public version is thin: the magic and the fire, with no people in it. Harry handed her the name to get rid of her; she was already digging into magic like the Mirror on his behalf, and comes back with a lead he never asked for.
  - **Dracaena is in it, as the guide.** She has the true version from family memory and nobody else in the castle does, which is what she is doing in the scene. She is not a dreamer here and does not go back to the painting — the compulsive returning still belongs to B6. What she brings is knowledge, snobbery and a verdict.
  - **The hunt.** The portraits are useless, because they despise painted life and won't say so. Dracaena asks a ghost instead, charms Sir Nicholas blind inside a minute, and has the location. First demonstration that she can work any room she likes — and cannot say a civil word to the boy she wants ([dracaena-malfoy](../characters/dracaena-malfoy.md)).
  - **The slur.** Hermione's delight at a marvellous extinct magic is, to her, a stranger poking at her family's dead. See DIV-074.
  - **The argument.** Dracaena tells the true story; Hermione takes Renoir's side; Dracaena answers that Aline got what she was after and merely had to ration her time better. Harry hears both, wants Dracaena's to be true, and does not settle it — the creature does (DIV-072, Q-085).
  - **The painting.** Clea's landscape, fifth-floor corridor to the prefects' bathroom. The creature talks about his mistress in the present tense, describes a routine, and has no idea how long ago it stopped.
  - **The offer, and the refusal.** Harry offers to come back and visit. He is refused — *mistress will come* — because accepting him would mean admitting she won't. What puts him beyond help is his own hope, and it is Dracaena who tells Harry to stop trying. In B6 she is the one refusing the help.
  - **Her crack.** She ends it sad and glassy-eyed and walks off unsteadily, and Harry has no idea what he saw. First deposit in the account Q-035 draws on, and it must stay deniable — no visible crying.
  - **What Harry takes from it.** Two things. The reason he never again wants to live in a dream, demonstrated rather than asserted. And the harder one: magic like this reaches past the person who works it, and some things can't be taken back.
  - **The flaw.** He is shown it at eleven and still isn't careful. It comes due at Sectumsempra — an unknown spell, cast in anger, permanent damage (DIV-032). Never joined up in the text; the reader does it or doesn't.
- **Why:** it turns a B1 one-liner into the spine of the escapism theme and plants B5, B6 and B7 at once. Putting her in it costs nothing and buys three things: the warning reaches Harry from the girl who will make the same mistake, the reader meets her competence before her cruelty has an explanation, and the hand that shuts down his rescue in B1 is the one that refuses rescue in B6.

### DIV-074 · B1: the first Mudblood, and Harry doesn't know the word

- **Status:** draft
- **Canon:** Draco calls Hermione a Mudblood in B2, at the Quidditch pitch. Ron's fury and his backfiring curse are what teach Harry, and the reader, how bad the word is.
- **Ours:** it lands a year earlier, in the Dessendre corridor, and it has an argument inside it.
  - **The provocation.** Hermione, thrilled, calls the Dessendres interesting and says she would have loved to talk to them. To Dracaena that is a stranger treating her people's dead as a curio to be poked at and forgotten, and it is the one thing she cannot answer politely.
  - **What she says.** That this is why Potter should choose his friends more carefully; that these Mudbloods have no respect for traditions and accomplishments that are not theirs. The grievance is real and is aimed at Hermione's *method*, not her blood — which does not make it less of a slur, and is exactly why it isn't a cheap one. A live demonstration of DIV-008 out of an eleven-year-old's mouth.
  - **Nobody defends her.** Hermione goes still and digs her nails into Harry's arm. Harry feels it, gives her a worried look, files the whole thing under Malfoy being arrogant, and moves on — **because he does not know what the word means.** Canon has him learn it in B2 from Ron; here he simply hasn't got it yet, so the slur lands, hurts, and passes unanswered in front of him.
  - **The aftermath.** He finds out later, off the page or on it, and is furious with her — which to Dracaena is Potter turning on her for calling a spade a spade, the day after she thought they had got on. Another clumsy approach punished, and more fuel for the grudge. Who tells him and when: Q-092.
  - **Hermione says nothing about it.** She does not bring her own injury to the boy she has appointed herself to look after. That silence is the big-sister engine, not forbearance ([hermione-granger](../characters/hermione-granger.md)).

It also sets up B4, where the last one said in his hearing gets her bounced down a corridor and then told in a hospital bed that it was hideous (DIV-095) — and the promise she actually makes about his friends comes months later, after the Ball (Q-135) — and B6, where she goes back on it on purpose to make him hate her (DIV-015).

### DIV-075 · House-elves are houses, bound by a forgotten crime

- **Status:** settled. The texture is draft.
- **Canon:** House-elves are a sentient species in hereditary servitude, mostly content with it. Freeing one with clothes is a kindness. Hermione's campaign is played for comedy and the issue is never resolved.
- **Ours:** a house-elf is not a magical creature. It is a house.
  - **What they are.** Where one family line has lived in one house long enough, the house takes on the household's temperament and expresses it as a person — its upkeep, its warmth and its memory, walking about. An elf is a portrait of the family that made it, in character and in colouring. They do not reproduce and do not die in the ordinary way: a body can be destroyed and the house makes another.
  - **One house, one elf,** however many bodies it walks about in. Bodies scale with the demesne, which is why a manor keeps one and a castle holding a thousand children keeps a hundred. So there are a few dozen in Britain, and they index the decline: fewer houses, thinner lines, fewer bodies (DIV-035).
  - **None has been made since the binding.** Making a whole elf is the work of an unbroken line in one house over a span nobody has managed since, and the binding may have broken the capacity outright. Nobody knows, because nobody knows elves are made at all. Every elf alive is one of the originals, the population only shrinks, and no house in the story ever gets a new one (Q-097).
  - **Anchored, not confined.** Near-absolute power over the house and the things in it, including enlarging it if given material; no conjuring from nothing; always able to hear their own people call. Outside the demesne they weaken with distance.
  - **The house feeds them.** A housed elf eats nothing and never has: the body is real flesh and the house maintains it like its own roof. That is also why none has ever been paid — wages buy food and lodging, and the house is both. An **unhoused** elf has to eat like anything else — briefly true of every dismissed elf, true of Winky for eight months and of Dobby for five years — and food holds the body up while doing nothing for the spirit (Q-102).
  - **The keeping.** Originally they kept the house and protected the people in it because that is what they were for and what they wanted. The people are *the household*, never masters.
  - **And freedom is not what they want.** An elf is a spirit of the hearth: it exists to keep a house and the people in it, which is not a job it was handed but what it is made of, before the binding and after. Liberty as an end is a wizard's idea and about as meaningful to an elf as it would be to a fireplace. What an elf wants is a family worth nursing. So an elf asking to be freed is **asking to stop** — Dobby wants it because it kills him — and *freedom* is only the word everyone has to hand, wrong in his mouth, in Harry's and in Hermione's. It is why S.P.E.W. is named for the wrong thing and why her eventual answer is not a liberation (Q-100).
  - **The binding.** Long before Hogwarts, wizards bound them with dark magic so they could not refuse an order and could not leave. It took the obedience and never touched the will, which is the cruelty of it: the love is real, and is also a chain, and the elf cannot tell which is which. What is left of the will lives in the gaps between orders, at the price of self-punishment.
  - **The house is what grips.** The chain runs elf–house–household, and the house holds it.
  - **There is no seat.** The binding was cast once and what it sits on now is the elves: no artefact, no vault, nothing to find or destroy. The only instrument still standing is the house, so the seat of an elf's binding is a building and nothing wider — Grimmauld Place is Kreacher's. It is why force was never going to undo this, and why the answer has to be procedural (Q-100).
  - **The exit, and why nobody takes it.** A dismissed elf *can* live: a new household takes it, and it is re-anchored and bound properly again. Neither secret nor forbidden, and it has perhaps happened a handful of times in a thousand years, unrecorded, because both parties are disgraced by it. Two things keep it shut, both character rather than magic. First, **a dismissed elf does not want to live** — dismissal is the thing you are made of telling you that you are not worth housing, and the elf agrees. Second, **it would not ask even if it did.** **There is no key and we do not invent one:** what would have to change is an elf's mind, and it has happened exactly once, when Dobby changed his. Which is also why Winky could never have been saved, and why the corridor gives Hermione no procedure to copy (DIV-076, DIV-077, Q-100).
  - **The glitch.** Harry is a master with no house, which the curse has no provision for because nobody casting it imagined one. The hold catches wrong: a **partial bind**, anchored to a person and no demesne. It is why Dobby is feeble everywhere, why clothes do nothing to him now, why he could walk away any day and doesn't — and why it is slowly killing him, since a proper bind would have held him small and cheap and alive indefinitely. An oversight, not a loophole, and **a bug means the thing has seams**, which is the only real evidence anyone has that this magic can be worked at all (Q-100).
  - **The degradation.** They were near-human once, tall and fair. The binding dwindled them to what everyone knows. A bound elf is cheap to run; the true shape needs a whole house to sustain.
  - **The forgetting.** Both sides forgot, and the noble houses buried their shame so thoroughly that the cover-up was forgotten with the crime. Every account begins with elves already in service, as though they had always been furniture.
  - **Dissolution runs at the rate the elf lets go,** and nothing else. Dobby goes in minutes because he had wanted to go for decades; Winky takes eight months because she does not want to live and has not finished wanting things, so she erodes instead of releasing, conscious of it throughout. The quick death is the merciful one and almost nobody gets it. The reader sees the fast version first and only learns in B4 what dismissal ordinarily does (DIV-076, DIV-077).
  - **Nothing marks them.** A thousand years and not one elf grave, because housed elves do not die and nobody minds the ones that do. The nearest thing to a memorial is the row of heads at Grimmauld Place, mounted by the house whose bodies they were. Winky's is the first (DIV-077).
  - **What everyone "knows":** a dismissed elf does away with itself. Not the mechanism — nobody has that — but the fact, as folk knowledge, and it is near enough true. A freed elf comes apart *and wants to*, because dismissal is the thing you are made of telling you that you are not worth housing — fast if it is ready, over months if it is not. The one remedy is to ask another household to take you, and the shame of asking is what keeps the whole institution standing. Nobody is ignorant here. They have simply never asked why.
  - **Hogwarts.** Helga Hufflepuff brought her own bound elf and settled it into the castle to give it somewhere safe and warm, and it took root. That is the headstart, the hundred bodies, the nurturing character, and the cover story in miniature: a kindness laid over a crime by someone who meant well and never asked. The school is not a refuge and knows nothing.
- **Why:** [problems/house-elves.md](../problems/house-elves.md). It makes the institution a forgotten atrocity rather than an endorsed norm, which is what DIV-008 needs — the purebloods are custodians of a crime they did not commit and have never examined. It keeps elves out of the economy (DIV-035). And it gives the series its clearest statement of Harry's own want: an elf is needed and used and never wanted.

### DIV-076 · B2: Dobby begs, and Harry keeps him

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Harry tricks Lucius into freeing Dobby with a sock. Dobby is delighted, blasts Lucius down the corridor, and thrives as a free elf for the rest of the series.
- **Ours:** canon's trick stands exactly. What changes is what freedom turns out to be.
  - **Lucius discards it; Dobby catches it.** No temper, no aim, no malice: a nobleman strips a filthy Muggle sock off the diary and flings it aside as rubbish. The agency is Dobby's and what Lucius supplies is indifference, which is the whole charge against him. He also does not raise a hand to Harry — brawling with a twelve-year-old is beneath him, and canon's attack is cut.
  - **And he knew.** Everyone knows a dismissed elf does away with itself (DIV-075). What he does with that is stop at the end of the corridor and say something like *I hope you know what you have just done, Mr Potter*, then leave. Harry cannot read it, and neither can the reader, and both can come back to it.
  - **Canon's blasting is cut.** With no attack to answer, there is nothing for Dobby to defend.
  - **Dobby starts to go,** understands perfectly, and is calm about it. He is not begging and not bargaining: he has wanted to go for a long time. A hearth-spirit kept by a household it cannot love has nothing to be, and that is what he has been for longer than he can account for.
  - **Which is why he goes so fast.** Dissolution runs at the rate the elf lets go (DIV-075), and he had been at that door for decades without a way through it. Harry is talking him off a ledge rather than pulling him out of an accident — and that is one more reason the corridor does not extend to an elf who has not already decided (DIV-077).
  - **Harry understands what he has done, and cries.** This is the hinge, and it is not a mechanism. No lock opens: **Dobby changes his mind.** He had decided he was finished and meant it; a boy cries over him and offers him something, and he looks again and finds he is not quite done.
  - **Because there is suddenly something to hope for.** He remembers the thing he has always wanted — a great house full of a happy, kind family, Narcissa's dream worn by the house she lives in, which he does not know — and for the first time a version of it might actually happen. He cries himself, decides to give living another go, and asks. The shame of asking loses to wanting something.
  - **Harry accepts and refuses the word:** not master, friend. He means it as decency, not as a theory, and does not know the condition is the entire point, or that he qualifies only because he has no house for the curse to grip.
  - **The half-joke.** Harry points out that Dobby will be waiting a long while for a big happy family, seeing as he is twelve. It is a joke and a promise, and **he never delivers it** — Kreacher does (DIV-078).
  - **No witnesses.** The only working remedy in a thousand years happens in an empty corridor and is never reported.
  - **A partial bind, not a rescue.** The hold catches wrong, and that is what kills him over five years (DIV-075, DIV-050). Clothes stop working, so Harry could hand him a jumper daily to no effect, and Dobby could walk away whenever he liked. Every day he stays is chosen.
  - **Afterwards:** the Burrow, as lodging and not a demesne — Harry gives him the house he wishes were his own. The Manor goes cold from this night and nobody connects the two.
  - **Harry's vault is Dobby's.** Functionally complete access, never thought about twice. It keeps Winky in B4 (DIV-077), and it is how Harry finally repays the Weasleys, who will not take anything from him: a small room added on for himself that nobody asked for, the structure quietly shored up, repairs on transparently silly pretexts, all paid for and all built by Dobby. Molly cannot refuse a room for Harry, and Dobby gets to be the architect he has always wanted to be.
  - **And it feeds him.** He has never eaten in his life and is suddenly hungry, because the house that used to sustain him is gone (Q-102). The first meal is Molly's cooking at the Burrow in the summer of 1993 — a woman's work, by hand, for him — and he bawls his eyes out; Harry is in Diagon Alley that summer and only hears about it afterwards. Out of the yoke and fed, he feels better than he ever has, which is the false dawn: everyone reads it as freedom agreeing with him (DIV-050).
  - **Which is costing him.** Every improvement comes out of a thing with no house to draw on. Harry's generosity and Harry's homelessness are killing him together (DIV-050).
- **Why:** the remedy has to exist, be available to every elf alive, and never generalise. What stops it is despair first and shame second, which is character rather than mechanics, and the one time it is got past is one elf changing his mind — not a procedure, and nothing anyone could have learned from watching. It is also why Winky could never have been saved (DIV-077). It also leaves a working anomaly sitting in the Burrow for the one person clever enough to study it (Q-100).

### DIV-077 · B4: Winky dies, and Dobby can't save her

- **Status:** settled. Placement draft.
- **Canon:** Crouch sacks Winky at the World Cup. Dobby brings her to Hogwarts, where she refuses wages, drinks, mourns and deteriorates, and she is still alive at the end of the series. S.P.E.W. is comic relief.
- **Ours:** every canon beat stays on the page and only its meaning changes.
  - **The sacking is a death sentence and Crouch knows it.** He is not disposing of a witness; he is telling his elf to go and kill herself, in front of people who find it unremarkable.
  - **Her decline is dissolution.** Canon wrote the symptoms for us.
  - **Dobby spends the year trying to be Harry** — castle, warmth, a corner, meals — and fails, because none of it is a house, she will not beg, and he is barely an anchor himself. She will not take money from anyone either, so he keeps her out of Harry's vault, and Harry never notices he is paying for a second elf (DIV-076).
  - **Canon's wage from Dumbledore is cut.** Dobby never asks the school for work or pay. He has a person, which is all he has ever wanted; the castle needs nothing from anybody and has no work to give; and a Headmaster who employs freed elves makes Hogwarts a refuge in a small way, which this design cannot afford. The two of them are **the castle elf's guests** — it feeds whoever turns up, because that is what it was made for and no order has ever forbidden it. **Dumbledore does not know, or never mentions it.** It also keeps the vault the only money in the system, which is what Harry not noticing depends on.
  - **And there is no work for her.** Canon gives Winky chores and has her neglect them; here the chores were never real. She is the guest of a house that does not need her, with no house and no function, which is precisely the thing she cannot survive.
  - **She has to eat, and will not.** A dismissed elf is unhoused and therefore hungry, probably for the first time in its existence (Q-102), so refusing money is refusing food — taking either would be admitting she means to go on living. She drinks instead, and butterbeer is oblivion rather than sustenance. Dobby paying for her meals is forcing food on someone who means to starve.
  - **She dies,** in the lull after the Second Task, with **Harry, Ron, Hermione, Dobby — and Dracaena** there.
  - **She gets a grave, and it is Dobby's idea.** He asks Harry for it — an extraordinary thing for an elf to do, and the asking is the point. He has been turning his own mortality over since the corridor, without knowing it is already under way, and he has decided that Winky should be honoured. **It is the first elf grave in Britain** (DIV-075). Harry digs it, and the others are there.
  - **And it is why Dobby has one.** In B7 nobody asks and Harry buries him anyway, because of this. What Dobby arranged for himself without knowing it, he got (DIV-050).
  - **Dracaena watches a freed elf die,** which no pureblood ever does. Beforehand she tells Harry it is his responsibility to provide for his own elf and that he had better be **a better master** — noblesse oblige, the code she was raised in, and the only advice anyone gives all year that would actually keep an elf alive. Then she stands in the kitchen for the end of it. The stories she was raised on say the elf *goes away*; it is a suicide over eight months, and that is where her class's account of elves stops working for her. She does not change sides and makes no argument of it (Q-083) — but she is the one pureblood in Britain who has stood in the room, and it is why she can end up backing Hermione's method, quietly, long after the war (Q-098, Q-100).
  - **It is where Hermione's dislike of Crouch settles into hatred.** She has disliked him since the World Cup (canon); here it stops being political. She has watched a man's discarded servant take eight months to stop existing because he told her she was not worth housing, and she is the one person in the castle who extends him no sympathy through the back half of the book.
  - **Hermione learns what freeing does early,** and from Harry rather than an enemy: he says he *has* an elf, never *owns* — a word he does not think in — she is appalled, and Dobby turns up without being summoned because he feels **Harry** is in a pinch, Hermione having hounded him into one. Her first-hand account of elf life comes from the elf.
  - **S.P.E.W. stops being comic,** and how much of it survives is open. If the hats stay they are traps — but she is not the castle's household, so they may have done nothing at all, and nobody can tell her (Q-093, Q-099).
- **Why:** it proves the B2 remedy doesn't generalise, three books before Dobby's death, using almost no new page time in the book that is already fullest. It also gives the reader the rule so that B7 is a recognition rather than an explanation.

### DIV-078 · B7: Grimmauld dies, and Kreacher colonises the Manor

- **Status:** draft. The mechanism is undesigned (Q-100).
- **Canon:** Kreacher is transformed by the trio treating him kindly once they learn about Regulus, and leads the Hogwarts elves into the Battle of Hogwarts. Harry's living arrangements are never addressed.
- **Ours:** canon's warming stands and cannot be the finish, because **Kreacher is already completely Harry's** — bound to Grimmauld through Sirius's will, with Harry occupying the Black position. Being given to Harry and being treated well are both already true and have changed nothing.
  - **The feud.** Dobby and Kreacher meet at Grimmauld, earlier than canon's meeting. Kreacher is a house that has never been anything else, looking at a thing with no house that is becoming something else, and the contempt is horror. How the feud opens is not settled.
  - **He can see Dobby is dying,** alone of everyone, and says so constantly as an insult. Nobody listens, because he is Kreacher. After Christmas, Harry remembers he was told.
  - **The way out is Hermione's, and it is a method.** Not freeing, which kills them — a way for a bound elf to recover its natural state over time, as a house-spirit, in a household that has decided never to use what it holds. Slow, partial, procedural, and the first real thing anyone has done about this in a thousand years. She is not powerful and the method must not need power; she finds the seam rather than beating the magic. Undesigned: **Q-100**, and it is her whole S.P.E.W. questline.
  - **What it is not:** a repeat of Harry's accident. That worked once, on one elf, because the two of them were the right people at the right moment on the right seam, and Hermione sees that it cannot be extended. She takes the corridor as proof the magic has seams and goes to find a different one. There is also nothing to break — the binding sits on the elves, not in an artefact, and the only instrument still standing is the house (DIV-075).
  - **The cost is houses, permanently.** Performing it ends the house the elf leaves, which is why Grimmauld dies mechanically rather than sentimentally. Old houses are the only thing that ever made elves and are already disappearing (DIV-035), so every elf saved costs one of the few left and the method cannot be scaled without finishing what the ancient wizards started. It also hands the purebloods their best genuine grievance — a Muggle-born dismantling the oldest houses in Britain, for house-elves — and she is right anyway (Q-100).
  - **Kreacher is the prototype**, at Malfoy Manor, after the war. His cost is consent: leaving Grimmauld means giving up the house of Black, the wall of heads and everything he has ever been, on the word of a Muggle-born.
  - **Grimmauld Place dies** when its spirit walks out, which is also his verdict on the Blacks.
  - **The ending.** Harry and Dracaena live at **Malfoy Manor** — she would not abandon her ancestral house, Grimmauld is dead, and the Manor has stood empty of itself since Dobby and will never make another elf (Q-097). So a foreign elf fills it: a Black elf keeping a Malfoy house, which Lucius has to live with and which by Kreacher's own snobbery is entirely proper, Narcissa having been a Black. He spends decades doting on Potter half-bloods and would die before admitting it, and he is the only one who ever knew Dobby was dying, so the remembering falls to him.
- **Why:** canon's kindness has already happened and already failed, so the ending has to come from somewhere else. Routing it through Hermione gives her research a destination, gives S.P.E.W. an actual arc, and keeps the hope procedural and slow instead of magical — which is the only kind of hope DIV-008 can carry.

### DIV-079 · Minerva "Hellfire" McGonagall

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** A member of the Order of the Phoenix in the second war. Canon never places her in the first one — she is not in the photograph of the original Order — and gives her no combat record at all before the Battle of Hogwarts. Two years at the Ministry, then Hogwarts from 1956. Nothing about her is unsettling.
- **Ours:** in the first war she was the Ministry's special-operations asset, not a spy. She led raids on Death Eater houses and gatherings with squads of Aurors, using her signature spell Lustrum (DIV-080), and the other side called her **Hellfire**.
  - **They had her long before the war.** Canon gives her two years at the Ministry and then Hogwarts from 1956, which we keep — she is a teacher, not an employee. The Ministry simply never took her off the books, and called her in for work abroad through the fifties, sixties and after. So 1970 is not a recruitment; it is the Ministry reaching for something it already owned.
  - **And why a schoolmistress rather than an Auror.** Lustrum is castable only from a narrow window (DIV-080), and the Aurors are past it: authorised to use Unforgivables in that war, and carrying a career of curse damage besides. She was the only person in Britain clean enough to light the fire and dirty enough to catch.
  - **How it reaches the page: the remarks.** Obscure, funny, unhinged asides dropped into ordinary teaching, which nobody dares follow up. Foreign names are the early work, British ones the war, and some of them come back later as Patronus battle honours. Full device in her file.
  - **The garb and the scars.** Singed battle dress she has never repaired and burns she has never had treated, because Lustrum always takes its price out of the caster. She calls it penance.
  - **What she actually is.** A battle junkie who hides it almost perfectly. Her Patronus runs on her raids, and she names each one to herself as she casts — a place and a date, like a battle honour. She never says them aloud: the dates are interior and rare, and the public face of the same history is the remarks (Q-107, answered).
  - **The penance is not for the killing.** It is for having loved it. The charm works because the joy was real.
  - **Discipline as a cage.** It is why she is the strictest teacher in the castle. When the cage opens in B3 she laughs, and the whole school hears it. Afterwards she denies the night flatly and threatens detention to anyone who raises it, which only feeds the legend.
  - **Strix palace.** Late in the first war she led the raid that destroyed the Strix household and killed Basil Thynne. Arvilla Strix and her infant son survived, which under Lustrum's rule was arithmetic rather than luck: it marked her, and found nothing at all in a child who had never cast a thing (DIV-080, DIV-082).
- **Why:** it gives the series' most reliable adult a real war behind her, makes the Patronus charm say something uncomfortable about what counts as a happy memory, and puts a person on the staff who can argue the Azkaban question from experience ([problems/azkaban.md](../problems/azkaban.md)).

### DIV-080 · Dementors can be destroyed

- **Status:** settled. Q-103 and Q-104 are closed by this entry.
- **Canon:** nothing destroys a Dementor. A Patronus drives them off, and that is the limit. They guard Azkaban as a fact of nature, and the heroes never address it.
- **Ours:** they are a dangerous magical creature, not a fixture of the universe. They can be hunted and destroyed, and the Ministry knows it. The precedent is canon's own: wizards hunted the British giants to nothing inside a century. Nobody has done it to the Dementors because nobody has decided to.
  - **Lustrum is not the only way — it is McGonagall's way.** It is what she specialises in, and it is the method the books put on the page. Deliberately left open: this is not a second lock on the Dementors.
  - **Lustrum**, a cousin of Fiendfyre. Incantation *Lustra nefas*, after the Roman censor's rite of purification.
  - **It is not a judgement, and the belief that it is one is propaganda.** Everyone, including most of those who have cast it, believes Lustrum weighs a person and burns what they owe. What it does is scorch everything Dark-touched inside the burn, in proportion, and nothing else. **It counts spells.** The gap between the legend and the mechanism is the design, and it runs from the origin story to the name of the vicewick.
  - **What it burns is Dark magic present in a person, however it got there** — the residue the Dark Arts leave when they crack the soul (Q-027). Working it is the commonest route in, not the rule: a curse somebody else put on you leaves the same substance, which is why Dark wounds never properly heal. **So it cannot tell a perpetrator from a victim,** any more than it can tell a torturer from a man who hexed his neighbour. An Auror wearing a career's worth of curse damage burns hotter than a minister who has never been hit by anything. The war leaves marks on the good ones too, and the fire reads the marks.
  - **The wounded are the most flammable people on a battlefield.** Cursed scars, a Dark Mark, a fragment of somebody else's soul — all of it catches, none of it chosen. There is no casual use of Lustrum near casualties in any book.
  - **The stain is on the casting; the spell's design decides how easy the question is.** Dark magic runs on intent (DIV-084), so a spell built only to afflict cannot be cast cleanly, and a spell with uses that are not harm — Expelliarmus, Stupefy, Protego, Petrificus Totalus, Impedimenta — can be. No fixed register of Dark spells is needed.
  - **So nobody is clean and small children are.** Every student above first year has thrown a curse; an ordinary person in the burn is scorched rather than spared. A child has had no opportunity, which is not the same as an innocent nature. Grass, trees and stone are untouched for the same reason.
  - **It leaves the Dark behind.** The fire runs on what it finds and destroys the person, so what remains of anyone burned is the worst of what they ever cast, concentrated, scalding for months. It absolves nobody and settles nothing: it inflicts a punishment.
  - **The caster must be flammable.** The price is intrinsic and comes out of them on the spot, as burns shaped like a grip. *(Answers Q-104.)*
  - **Which leaves a window, and it is the safety catch.** Too clean and it will not light — anyone who has never worked Dark magic cannot cast Lustrum at all, so the admission ticket is having crossed the line. Too dirty and it kills you, the caster being the nearest fuel. **Voldemort could not cast this, nor Bellatrix, nor Macnair**, which is why the other side never reaches for the best area weapon in the country. And since Lustrum is itself Dark, every casting deposits more and walks the caster toward the far edge: a clock, whose only warning is that the backlash keeps growing. It is what finished Appius.
  - **It cannot overcome magic stronger than the caster's,** where the gate is power invested in a single piece of work rather than category. An Inferius is cheap per unit and burns readily; a Horcrux or a great Dark artefact is not, so the fire catches and cannot consume, burning the vessel and leaving the thing inside — **you would get the man and not the thing in him.** The Horcrux hunt is therefore untouched. DIV-025 stands for a separate reason: she cannot drop a Lustrum in a hall full of her own people, who are flammable too. *(Answers Q-103; the earlier "invisible to it" proposal is struck.)*
  - **A siege weapon, and an offensive one.** You drop it on a position you do not occupy, which is why the war record is raids and why a defender can almost never use it. It does not pick sides, and in a war everybody's hands are dirty, so allies are fuel.
  - **Nobody knows what it is.** Not in the open stacks: Hermione finds nothing in B3 and gets at it in B4 through the Restricted Section, with Flitwick's permission and supervision, because he would rather choose which true thing she walks away with than let her guess or go round him (Q-112). The Ministry regulates the incantation, which is how Fudge can call its use unauthorised.
  - **The vicewick.** The enhancer that turns one casting into the small sun over Hogwarts. It is rendered ash — what Lustrum leaves of what it has burned, the only substance that is nothing but Dark magic — distilled and set in a vessel that offers the fire a nearer meal than the caster. It does not make the spell safe; it misdirects it. Supply is capped by the near-total absence of anyone who can cast, so there are no stockpiles. The name flatters the legend: it says the thing holds a man's vices. Flitwick makes McGonagall's without ever practising Dark magic — a sleight of hand, not a corruption.
  - **The policy.** The Ministry keeps the Dementors because they are useful. McGonagall is a loud and unpopular proponent of exterminating them.
- **Why:** it converts Azkaban from scenery into a choice somebody is making, and gives the pureblood side a documented grievance that is not a lie told by losers (DIV-008, Q-016). The propaganda layer sharpens the charge: the Ministry did not outsource its judgement to a spell, it burned houses on the word of an instrument that measures one thing, and called the output a verdict.

### DIV-081 · B3: the Dementors storm Hogwarts

- **Status:** settled (the event). Draft: where it sits in the night, which waits on Q-003.
- **Canon:** the Dementors converge on the lake, are driven off by Harry's Patronus, and that is the end of them. The night finishes with the Time-Turner rescue and Sirius's escape from the tower.
- **Ours:** Harry's Patronus does not merely drive the swarm off, it **maims** it. Wounded and starving, the horde turns on the nearest thousand souls and tries to force its way into the castle. **Nothing restrains it**, because nothing ever did: the garrison was an arrangement the Ministry mistook for control (DIV-084), and Fudge siccing them at the lake was a request.
  - **The staff hold the walls** with their Patronuses. McGonagall transfigures her battle garb onto herself in front of the school; Flitwick gives her back the enhancer she made him hold and covers her; she goes up and breaks the siege with Lustrum.

  - **The legend.** The school never gets an explanation, and the night becomes one of Hogwarts's stories.
- **Why:** it makes Harry's Patronus an event with consequences rather than a rescue, pays off a garrison that has hung over the whole book, and solves the climax's logistics problem — the chaos Dracaena caused is what lets her finish the job (Q-003, Q-025).

### DIV-083 · The Dementors are not one thing

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** an undifferentiated mass. They serve the Ministry through the first war and change sides only after Voldemort returns, which does not track — a decade in Azkaban while he was at his strongest, then a switch within two years of his coming back. Canon also gestures at them having feelings and communicating among themselves, and never follows it up.
- **Ours:** they have factions, and the first war split them.
  - **Some went over to Voldemort, and not all of them.** Azkaban keeps running through the war on a reduced contingent, so the prison does not have to be explained away, and Voldemort's side gets a real force out of it. It also removes canon's worst piece of timing: the scary demons had no reason to wait.
  - **The defection comes first and the herding comes after. This order is load-bearing.** They went to Voldemort willingly, for open country and unrestricted feeding, which Azkaban does not offer. Only then did he find he had a mob rather than a force, and ask Lucius whether anything existed for herding them — which is what sends Lucius into the family holdings and ends in Macnair discovering what *Arceo* does when you mean it badly (DIV-084). What that bought was not their loyalty, which he already had, but their *discipline*: a swarm turned into an army, deployable and aimable. And they took to it, because disciplined work fed them better than wandering did, which is why they stay disciplined long after anyone stops casting.
  - **The ones who stayed are not loyal. They are gourmands.** Azkaban's despair is a particular vintage — concentrated, renewable, served daily — and they have developed a taste for it. There is nothing noble in staying and it is worse than defecting, because it is a far heavier charge against Azkaban than loyalty would ever have been: the things that guard it *prefer* it.
  - **What became of the defectors.** McGonagall and others like her destroyed a great many. The remnants were sent back to Azkaban, because they are an asset and this Ministry does not throw assets away. How they were put back: DIV-084.
  - **Which means the garrison in B3 may be those same things**, taken back into service. That is what makes McGonagall's restraint at the Quidditch match enormous: she is standing twenty feet from her own war, carrying the one thing that could end it, with the school behind her.
- **Why:** it fixes canon's chronology, supplies the occasion for Lustrum's reputation as the Dementor-killer (which "she burned Death Eater houses" does not), gives Azkaban its single worst fact, and turns McGonagall's objection to the garrison from squeamishness into an argument with evidence behind it.

### DIV-084 · Arceo

- **Status:** settled (the spell and the chain). Draft: how much reaches the page in B4, and when Dracaena learns it (Q-109).
- **Canon:** in B5 Fudge insists the Dementors cannot leave Ministry control, and the books never say what that control consists of. Voldemort has them anyway. Lucius walks free in 1981 on an Imperius defence nobody examines. And in B3 the Dementors repeatedly do as they please — the train, the match, the lake — which canon never reconciles with being under anyone's command.
- **Ours:** one spell, with one incantation, which does what the caster wants it to do.
  - **Incantation: *Arceo*.** First person singular, like *Accio* and *Protego*. It means *to ward off, keep at a distance* — and, in the same breath, *to shut in, enclose, confine*. One verb for both halves of the spell's life, which is the point: **there is no second spell.** There never was a curse and a clean version. There is *Arceo*, and there is what you want while you cast it (Dark Magic runs on intent: [magic](../setting/magic.md)).
  - **A Malfoy ancestor made it, as a defence.** Something that gets under a Dementor's skin and makes it want to be elsewhere: a way to push them off a house, off a road, off a child. Clean magic, decent purpose.
  - **Lucius found it because Voldemort asked.** Voldemort had Dementors and they were all over the place; he wanted them herded, and herding is procurement, which is Lucius's whole function. He went through the family holdings and came back with his great-great-grandfather's charm. He did not make it and did not see what else it was.
  - **Macnair saw.** Hearing it described, he realised the same spell that says *go away* would say *come here* if you meant it that way, and he meant it that way. No scholarship required — just the appetite, which he has. Voldemort is delighted: he has not gained Dementors, he has gained orders.
  - **It is upkeep, not ownership.** *Arceo* is a continuous influence and nothing else. You do not cast it on a Dementor and keep it. Stop casting and it walks away whenever it likes. A **place** can be warded permanently, because the enchantment sits in the stone — that is how Azkaban's human guards survive and how Ministry buildings stay habitable. **A creature cannot be owned.**
  - **So why do they stay?** Because the terms are good. Macnair and the Death Eaters treated them *well*, and they worked out that taking orders nets more food than wandering does. They are not slaves and never were. They are there voluntarily, and they would leave for a better offer — which is exactly what Voldemort eventually makes them.
  - **The amnesty.** Lucius sells the Ministry the charm, as the price of the Imperius story being believed, and it goes into ordinary use as warding. **Macnair comes in with no position at all** — not a liaison, not a department, nothing on paper, because the Ministry has no intention of publicising that its Dementors answer to a man rather than a policy. Officially he is the executioner. What he actually does is the fine work: he is better at *Arceo* than anyone else alive, and gets precision where others get crude herding.
  - **Which makes Fudge wrong, not dishonest.** The Dementors can leave Ministry control any time they care to. They don't, because Azkaban feeds them and the arrangement has been comfortable for fourteen years. He has mistaken a standing offer for a chain, and so has everyone else.
  - **And it explains B3.** Nobody is holding the garrison. There is no leash on the grounds, only an arrangement, and an arrangement does not cover what happens when a thirteen-year-old maims the horde at the lake. Fudge *sics* them: that is a request. Afterwards, wounded and starving, nothing whatsoever is restraining them (DIV-081).
  - **The delivery is the B4 Pensieve.** Harry watches Karkaroff buy his freedom with names; in the same hearings the shape of Lucius's bargain is said out loud, in passing, in language Harry has no use for yet. **Dracaena does not know.** She has the Imperius story and she believes it.
- **Naming, in-world and ours.** The registers call it the Arceo Charm; in departmental use it is just warding. **The other use has no name**, because naming it would mean admitting to it — so there is no file on it anywhere, which is precisely why it keeps being rediscovered by whoever is willing (DIV-085). "The leash" and "the collar" are *our* shorthand in this bible and must never reach the page.
- **Why:** it turns Fudge's B5 line from a plot hole into a misapprehension with a date on it; it gives Voldemort's hold on the Dementors a mechanism that is an offer rather than a chain, which is where canon itself ends up; it explains why the Dementors disobey all through B3; and it gives Dracaena's father a third charge sheet, heavier than Dobby and Buckbeak, that she has not read.

### DIV-086 · Moody is Moody

- **Status:** settled (the mechanism and the year). Draft: the clue chain (Q-114). What the year leaves in Harry is DIV-096; the ferret is DIV-095.
- **Canon:** Barty Crouch Jr takes Alastor Moody, keeps him in his own seven-locked trunk for ten months, and holds his shape on hourly Polyjuice for a whole school year without Dumbledore, McGonagall or Snape noticing. Moody is consequently a non-character: everything the reader likes about the B4 teacher belongs to a Death Eater, and the real man turns up half-starved in the last chapter.
- **Ours:** it is the real Moody all year. He is under the Imperius Curse, and **Crouch is in the trunk**, under the Draught of Living Death.
  - **The sleep is the design, not the hiding place.** Suspended, Crouch neither eats, drinks nor sleeps, and has no body to run. His entire existence for ten months is holding one man down, a few feet away, constantly.
  - **Which is why the curse holds on the one man in Britain who teaches resisting it.** Not because Moody is weaker than his reputation. Because Crouch is exactly as able as canon makes him, wholly undistracted, and insane. Moody surfaces; Crouch is always there to put him back. The hold is maintained, not cast once — so Crouch *has* to be in the castle, which is what makes the trunk necessary rather than decorative.
  - **Voldemort's contribution is a refinement, not the curse.** He gives Crouch a way to *wear* the man as well as steer him: his own speciality, possession, fitted to somebody else's Imperius. Crouch cast it, Crouch holds it, Crouch rides it. The master supplied the technique and nothing else.
  - **Holding and wearing are the same grip.** He can hold Moody down or wear him, not both at full strength. Every take-over loosens the hold, so every take-over is a calculated risk — which is why there are so few, and why the end happens the way it does.
  - **The take-overs:** the name into the Goblet under a fourth school; the Imperius on Crouch Sr, and the murder on the grounds in May; the Cup turned into a Portkey before the maze is sealed; the attempt on Harry in the office afterwards. Wormtail minds Crouch Sr off-site, as in canon, so Moody never has to leave the castle.
  - **The ferret is the unforced one.** Crouch hates the Death Eaters who stayed free far more than he hates anybody on Dumbledore's side, and he takes over to bounce Lucius Malfoy's daughter down a corridor. No plan value, spite only. It is the fair-play clue and the ending in miniature: the same appetite that nearly exposes him in the first weeks of term is the thing that destroys him in June. **She is cursing Hermione Granger when he does it**, not Harry, and Harry carries her out of the corridor afterwards (DIV-095).
    - **And it is the one cruelty Harry forgives him for.** He files it under *that is how Moody does things* and likes the man regardless — so the single act of the year Harry has to excuse on Moody's behalf is the only one that was not Moody's. The real man then has to live with having transfigured a student and thought it just (Q-117).
  - **He spends the year keeping Moody's mouth shut.** Moody remembers putting the boy's name in the Goblet and remembers having had a reason at the time (that is the Imperius working as canon describes it), so he would simply *say so* if it came up — and he keeps nearly saying so. Crouch has to take him over for a split second, again and again, to stop him. **On the page it reads as Moody opening his mouth about the culprit and then shrugging it off,** a hard old man thinking better of a theory; on a reread it is a man being silenced mid-sentence, all year.
    - **Every one of those is a loosened grip** (see above), which is why the hold is frayed by June and why the office finally goes wrong. He has been spending his margin on this since October.
    - **The confession pays it off in one line.** Under Veritaserum, Crouch says something like *I had to keep his voice down all year — he was dying to tell them he put the name in.* Nothing else needs saying; the reader recontextualises every shrug at once.
  - **He does not touch the Tournament.** Fleur is never Stunned out of the maze and Krum is never Imperiused. Intervening in front of a crowd is exposure he cannot afford and grip he cannot spare — so he uses the teaching post instead and makes Harry as formidable as he can. **He is not required to deliver a winner.** His orders are to make the Cup a Portkey; Potter winning it is the bonus his master would like, and the teaching is how he buys a better chance at it. **It also serves the base plan directly**, which is the nastier half: the fourth champion was entered to make the pot big enough, and the fourth champion is fourteen, so every Task Harry pours himself into is hoard that would not otherwise be there (DIV-087). Fleur and Krum keep their dignity, and Harry keeps his victory.
  - **There is no lost time.** The Imperius supplies the reason along with the compliance: Moody remembers putting a name in the Goblet and remembers having thought it sound. His horror at the end is that he remembers wanting to.
  - **Everything else in the year is his own.** The lessons, the drills, the coaching, the paranoia, the flask, the eye. The man who taught Harry really did teach Harry. **The flask is spent deliberately**, after the First Task, by being poured for somebody else (DIV-097).
  - **Moody takes himself back.** In Dumbledore's office Crouch rides him too hot — appetite is the one thing he has never governed — the grip slackens, and Moody uses that instant to throw him out. The hand on the wand wavers; that is the whole beat. It is also the reader's proof of what he is, which canon never gives them, and it sets up what he can do in the Order.
  - **Fudge has Crouch Kissed in custody.** Not a Dementor loose in the castle and not a panicked bungle. Crouch confesses under Veritaserum in front of Dumbledore, McGonagall and Snape, is taken away by the Ministry, and is Kissed days later on the Minister's order. Fudge deliberately destroys the one witness who could corroborate Harry, which is where B5's campaign starts — and Dumbledore, who let him be taken, owns that. It also keeps a Dementor well out of McGonagall's reach (DIV-080, DIV-083).
- **Why:**
  - **Canon's version is the least defensible thing in the book**, and ours is the one thing canon has already established as undetectable: Rosmerta runs a pub under Dumbledore's nose for most of B6, and the entire 1981 amnesty exists because nobody can test for the Imperius. There is nothing to catch here, because Moody's oddity is authentic.
  - **It gives Moody back to the books.** His B4 teaching is his, so his B5–B7 self is continuous with the man Harry loved, instead of his characterisation hanging off somebody doing an impression of him.
  - **It shrinks the conspiracy to almost nothing.** Voldemort needs Harry to survive and win; so does Moody. The best teacher in Britain does the plan's work sincerely and for free, and Crouch is left with five acts in ten months.
  - **And B4 is the Imperius book.** Harry watches the old hearings in the Pensieve in the same year, and Lucius walked free on a defence nobody could examine. The book now demonstrates that the greatest wizards alive cannot tell, which makes Lucius's story unfalsifiable on the page, in front of his daughter, who believes it (DIV-084, Q-109).

### DIV-087 · The Goblet of Fire: the charge and the contract

- **Status:** settled, including the fourth champion as arithmetic. The finish — the maze as an elimination and the duel at the Cup — is DIV-089.
- **Canon:** the Goblet picks champions and binds them to compete, and "binding magical contract" is never given any content. The prize is a thousand Galleons and glory. Voldemort runs a year-long conspiracy and a whole Tournament to obtain a boy whom Crouch could have taken off a Hogsmeade street — or simply pricked — and the stated reason, that he wants Harry taken from under Dumbledore's nose and his death made to look like an accident, is the weakest part of the book.
- **Ours:** the Tournament generates power, the Goblet holds it, and the ritual needs it.
  - **The charge.** Three of the finest young witches and wizards alive spend a year pouring themselves into three lethal Tasks. The Goblet catches what would otherwise bleed away, and the hoard grows Task by Task, peaking when the Cup is claimed. **Nothing is taken from anybody**: the losers go home as they came, and the pot was never theirs — it is what the contest made.
  - **The prize is the pot,** absorbed by the victor and kept for life: the difference between a good wizard and a great one, a lifetime's practice handed over in an afternoon. Which is why children enter a competition with a death toll, and why past champions are remembered for what they did afterwards.
  - **Lethal is the yield.** You cannot harvest a safe tournament, so the danger is the instrument rather than tradition — and the 1994 committee had to find the yield without the corpses, which makes their choices culpable rather than merely old-fashioned. **A reformed Tournament makes a smaller pot, and that is the hinge the whole plan turns on** (the fourth champion, below).
  - **The spectators are extra eyes, and nothing more** *(Q-119)*. The Goblet has to witness a contribution to count it — that is what the judges and the scores are for — and a crowd is more of the same: a great many small judges, feeding it a marginal amount of what it already gets from the panel. **It is not a battery and the crowd is not a resource**, or the instrument could be fed by holding a rally. Nothing is taken from them either, same rule as the champions. It does mean the relay (DIV-088) is part of the instrument rather than a patch, and it gives the 1994 committee one honest line of defence: they lowered the lethality and made a little of it back on the gate.
  - **Why it was abolished, and who won it before** *(Q-120)*. The published reason is the death toll, and the cockatrice is the incident that let them close it. The real reason has a name, and nobody could say it out loud without saying what the prize is. Written out in [triwizard](../setting/triwizard.md): Étienne Bouchard, Loxias, Serena Passel, and the escalation that comes built into any instrument whose yield is danger.
  - **It is awarded raw and takes minutes to settle.** Until it settles it can be drawn out of the victor; afterwards it is simply part of them. That window is the whole plan.
  - **It takes one claimant.** It will not split the pot and does not recognise a tie, and **claiming is not the same as touching**: the charge goes to whoever claims the Cup, while a Portkey carries whoever is in contact with it. The two come apart in the maze, and the inch between them is why Cedric Diggory is the spare (DIV-089).
  - **The forfeit is collection, not punishment.** A bound champion owes a contribution; one who competes pays it by competing; one who refuses cannot, so the Goblet takes it out of the body — **all of it, not a year's worth.** A champion who will not commit is stripped of their magic entirely and banished from the wizarding world, no ifs and no buts. It is not angry and is not making an example. It is balancing a ledger. **And it is a debt settled, not a windfall:** what it takes in forfeit goes to squaring the account, not into the pot. Stripping a wizard is what it costs to collect from somebody who will not pay any other way; none of it is yield. **So a field entered in order to refuse would make nothing**, and the only way to raise a charge is still to hold a contest. **It knows the difference between being stopped and giving up,** and will not accept a champion going through the motions, which is what the judges and the scores are actually for: they witness contribution. Fleur dragged out of the lake by grindylows has paid.
  - **It is exactly what it says it is.** No secret, no Dark under-layer, no loophole Dumbledore failed to find: an impartial arbiter with no mercy and no discretion, which is why it will take a fourteen-year-old's magic without blinking and does not care that he was entered falsely. Its honesty is the horror. Deliberately *not* built as legend-versus-mechanism — that shape is already Lustrum's and *Arceo*'s (DIV-080, DIV-084), and a third would be a tic.
  - **So the plan is the Tournament — and it is deliberately not all-in.** What Voldemort wants most is a body, and the Goblet's hoard is too good an occasion to let pass. The ritual needs an enemy's blood and a charge to burn in the same body, and **the champion supplies both, whoever the champion turns out to be.** The charge peaks at the Cup and must be taken before it settles; the Cup is the one object guaranteed to be held by the victor at that instant; the Portkey is the only vector there is.
  - **The blood clause is barely a constraint, and canon says so.** Blood of the enemy, taken by force — and any wizard living who would stand against him will do, which is very nearly all of them. **Harry is wanted for one particular reason:** he is the way past the protection in his own skin. That is an upgrade, not a requirement.
  - **So Crouch's orders are one sentence: send me the Goblet's magic.** Make the Cup a Portkey before the maze is sealed, and whoever claims it arrives in the graveyard with the charge still loose in them. **One man can guarantee that.** No man could guarantee a fourteen-year-old winning a tournament, which is exactly why the plan never asks him to.
  - **The fourth champion is arithmetic first and vanity second.** Voldemort does the sum on a Tournament that has had the corpses taken out of it, and three champions in a de-lethalised contest will not make a pot worth burning in the ritual. **He needs a fourth contributor: the field has to be bigger because the danger is smaller.** A fourth school is the only way to get one, and only one name can go under it — so, having to enter somebody, he enters Potter. **The slot is the requirement; the name in it is the indulgence.**
    - **And once is all the Goblet will wear.** Confound it about the shape of the field and it accepts what it is told; do it twice and it throws the whole contest out. There is no version with eight champions and a bigger pot, which is why nobody ever ran one. **Crouch says so flatly in the confession**, and that is where the reader gets the arithmetic.
    - **It changes nothing for Harry, and is not meant to.** He was not picked for being special — the slot existed either way and a fourth child was going into that maze whatever happened — but that is not what his guilt is made of. **What he cannot get past is that he did not get out of it**, and a boy died because he stayed in (DIV-092). The arithmetic is for the reader, and no character is ever to offer it to him as consolation.
    - **Which makes the teaching part of the base plan, not a flourish.** A fourteen-year-old is the weakest contributor he could have picked and he picked him anyway, so the pot he needs is now resting partly on a boy. Making Harry formidable no longer only shortens the odds of the premium outcome — **it feeds the hoard, Task by Task.** The best teacher in Britain spends the year fattening the harvest, sincerely and for nothing (DIV-086, DIV-105).
  - **Potter is still only the bonus, and the base plan still does not need him to win.** His name costs nothing the fourth slot was not already costing, and buys a chance at the premium outcome. If he loses, Voldemort rises anyway, out of somebody else. **Vain and petty, and still not a man who stakes everything on a boy winning a footrace** — he takes the win where it is going and keeps the floor underneath it.
  - **Which makes Cedric's arrival worse than it looks.** Had Cedric won alone, Cedric would have been the one bled, and Voldemort would have returned on schedule. He reaches the graveyard about a second too late to be the man they needed, and is killed for the surplus.
  - **The charge is spent as the price of the ritual.** Voldemort gains a body and nothing permanent; Harry is stripped of the one prize that would ever have mattered to him and keeps the gold he does not want (DIV-062). He is handed it at the exact moment it cannot save him — the greatest surge of power he will ever feel, arriving while he is bound to a headstone with a dead boy three feet away and his wand out of reach.
  - **And it leaves no mark on him afterwards** *(Q-122, dropped)*. It never settled, so there is nothing missing: no flatness, no sluggish magic, no convalescence. B5's Harry is angry because of what happened to him and not because of what was taken out of him, and the book must never be able to blame his magic for his temper. What survives is knowledge, not sensation — he is the only person alive who knows what the prize was, he had it for about ninety seconds, and it was burned to build a body. A sentence he can say once, to somebody who does not want to hear it.
  - **Dumbledore tells him plainly, in November,** what the contract will do to him and what he stands to win. Nothing to soften, so he does not soften it: honest, and still knowingly sending a fourteen-year-old into it.
  - **Why the threat works on Harry in particular.** Losing his magic means Privet Drive for good — his standing nightmare — and now it also means never seeing Dracaena again. The Tournament is the one door that stays open.
  - **It is also what makes the adults helpless** — and worse than they know. Dumbledore watches the trap close all year and cannot spring it, because the one counter available to him, making the boy lose, is precisely what the Goblet forbids. **And it would not have saved anybody**: the Cup was a Portkey for whoever took it, so a losing Harry only means a different child in the graveyard. Nobody on our side ever finds this out; the reader can.
  - **The charge never touches the Yule Ball.** It buys him nothing socially: strongest he has ever been, famous, scored, watched, hounded, and none of it reaches the one thing he wants. Sirius plants the idea and Harry asks her terrified (🔒 DIV-004, DIV-019). A version in which the charge supplies the nerve was proposed and binned by the author — a power-up must never cause the turning point of the series, or June takes it back. Where it shows instead is his casting, and **the first person to notice is Dracaena**, who knows his magic better than anyone after three years of the Duelling Club (DIV-028, DIV-021, Q-028).
- **Why:** it turns the book's biggest hole into the only plan available; it gives canon's famously empty "binding magical contract" content and teeth; it makes the adults' helplessness structural rather than authorial; it explains why three schools send children into something with a death toll; and it leaves Harry his own victory, which matters more than the pawn reading — he is the hero, and Voldemort wipes the floor with him in the graveyard regardless.

### DIV-088 · The Tasks are watched

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** the Second and Third Tasks are unobservable. The stadium watches the surface of a lake for an hour, and then the outside of a hedge, and the audience learns what happened when the champions come back out.
- **Ours:** both are relayed, so the school actually watches them.
- **Why:** canon's version is indefensible as spectacle, and we already depend on the audience having seen: Dracaena claims Harry at the lake in front of the whole school, and Fleur's pantomime three days later only works on a crowd that watched the Task (DIV-038). *(Q-119, settled)* **The spectators are extra eyes for the Goblet** — many small judges, witnessing what the panel witnesses, worth a marginal amount — so the relay is part of the instrument rather than a patch. Deliberately kept small: a crowd that mattered would be more interesting than the contest (DIV-087).

### DIV-089 · B4: the Third Task is a fight, and Harry wins it

- **Status:** settled. Open: exactly how he beats Krum (Q-123).
- **Canon:** the maze is an obstacle course the champions go through separately. Fleur is Stunned offstage, Krum is put under the Imperius Curse by Crouch, and Harry and Cedric help each other through and agree to take the Cup together. The tie is offered out of mutual decency, and canon's Harry does not especially want to win.
- **Ours:** **ours wants to win.** So does Cedric, and so do Fleur and Krum, because all four know what the prize actually is (DIV-087). The maze is an elimination, and the Cup has one claimant.
  - **She gives him permission to want it.** Not a motive and not an instruction — a wake-up call. Harry is planning to survive the Tournament; she tells him it is all right to want to make a splash, all right to want to win, and in fact he *should*, because he is the champion of Hogwarts in a tradition centuries old and ought to be proud of it. **It is what she would do, which is the whole of her reasoning: there is a chance, you take it.** Slytherin through and through, and the same ethic that made her approve of Krum's calculation at the World Cup (DIV-066).
    - **The wanting is his.** She removes the reason he was suppressing it; she does not supply it. He is nobody's puppet, least of all hers.
    - **When she says it is open, and the timing changes what it is** (Q-128). Before they are together it is a scathing remark from one of the very few people who believe he never entered himself — she has no reason left to disbelieve him, she will not wear a Potter Stinks badge, and he is already outstanding in her eyes; all she lacks is permission to let herself love him. After the Ball it is a loving one. The author can see both, and leans early.
  - **The Goblet takes one.** It will not split a charge and does not recognise a tie. Every champion knows it going in, so there is nothing to negotiate and nobody has to be talked out of sharing — and the reader watches the rule run twice before it matters.
  - **The maze herds.** Not a bracket anyone drew: it is an instrument for generating contribution, so of course it funnels four people towards one point. **The 1994 committee thought this was the safe option** — no dragons, no drowning, just trained champions and wands, and nobody dies in a duel. The safest Task they ever designed is the one that got a champion killed, somewhere else, for reasons none of them could have planned for ([triwizard](../setting/triwizard.md)).
  - **Nobody dares yield.** The Goblet knows the difference between being stopped and giving up, and nobody knows where that line sits. Testing it costs **everything** — all of your magic, and your place in the wizarding world with it — so the champions fight well past the point of sense, not because the rules say so but because none of them wants to find out. It is the floor under the appetite, not a replacement for it.
  - **Cedric beats Fleur.** She is faster and more inventive and she is beating him; he does not break. It is the same way he nearly beats Harry, and it stops the bracket reading as a ranking. Stopped twice in one year — grindylows in February, a Hufflepuff in June — and never once quitting: she is the champion who cannot be made to give up, wins nothing, and is the only one who walks out of the year undamaged.
  - **Harry beats Krum, outright and hard. This is the real final.** Krum was chosen and deserves it, and he is the hardest opponent in the maze by a distance; Harry is fourteen and wins anyway, and his edge is the one that beats Dracaena's technique: he has no shape (DIV-028, DIV-090). What he actually does is Q-123.
    - **Which quietly guts Harry's guilt later.** He will decide that if he had not been in the Tournament, Cedric would have won it (DIV-092). On the evidence of the maze that is almost certainly false — Krum would most likely have taken the Cup — and nobody ever puts it to him, because nobody is thinking about the bracket.
  - **Then Harry and Cedric, in front of the Cup.** Both riding the charge at its height, and neither will stand down — Harry first of all. He wins it fair: he is the better duellist.
  - **Why Harry will not stand down.** Her ribbon is on his wrist and he wants to show her what her knight is made of (DIV-058). B4 is the one book where he goes for the prize instead of giving it up, and the prize is for her.
  - **He takes a spell rather than let the ribbon burn, in the Krum fight.** He eats something he could have dodged, because dodging would have put her ribbon in its path — against the hardest opponent he has, which is what makes it cost. Dracaena watches it on the relay and is appalled, furious and flattered at once, all three at full strength and none of them cancelling the others out. **He takes damage to protect his lady's honour,** which is precisely what he does again in B5 when Montague says something about her and Harry ends his own Quidditch career over it (DIV-067).
  - **Why Cedric will not.** He beat Harry in B3 only because Harry fell off a broom in front of Dementors; Amos has been crowing about it since the walk to the Portkey in August (canon beat, kept); and Cedric is sick of it. He wants to hand his father something real instead. It is the rematch he offered in B3 and never got.
  - **And he does not stop when he loses.** Beaten, from the ground, he Summons the Cup — and Harry takes it out of the air, being also the better Seeker. Cedric catches his leg as the Portkey goes.
  - **Touch is not claim.** The Portkey carries whoever is in contact; the charge goes to whoever claimed. Cedric has a hand on Harry's ankle and not on the handle, and that inch is why he is the spare.
  - **They think the Task is still running.** The charge takes minutes to settle and is still churning in Harry, so neither boy reads the graveyard as the end of anything. The champions' misreading and Voldemort's window are the same fact.
  - **Their last exchange is about the bout.** Then canon: Wormtail, the green light, and Cedric dead before either of them has worked out where they are.
  - **The echo asks for two things** — take me back to my father, and well done. The congratulation is Cedric accepting that he lost, which is what keeps him from coming out of this any smaller than canon's. Harry can neither refuse it nor believe it.
  - **The school watched all of it** (DIV-088). Dracaena watched him win wearing her ribbon; Amos Diggory watched his son lose and then vanish; the judges witnessed the contribution, which is what judges are for; and then the relay went dead and stayed dead in front of the whole stand. Nobody thinks Harry killed him — they saw them both go — but several hundred people saw Harry beat Cedric Diggory minutes before Cedric died, and Rita Skeeter is in the building (Q-125).
  - **The Diggorys' visit, and what Harry makes of all this:** DIV-092. The gold comes out of that scene entirely.
- **Why:** canon's tie is the one place the book lets everybody off, and ours cannot afford it. The prize is a life's worth of magic, all four want it, and the Task is built to make them meet, so the finish has to be taken rather than shared — and the boy who takes it has to live with having wanted to. It also dissolves the mechanism problem instead of answering it: the Goblet never has to adjudicate two claimants, because it never gets two. And a maze the whole school is now watching (DIV-088) has to be worth watching, which four people wandering a hedge separately is not.

### DIV-090 · B4: Viktor Krum, the other famous boy

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Krum is background. Curt, trailed by fans, takes Hermione to the Ball, and is otherwise used as an obstacle — Imperiused in the maze, then in B5 the source of a compliment Hermione relays to talk Harry into teaching: that Harry could do things Krum could not, and Krum was in his seventh year. The book *tells* us Harry is formidable rather than showing it.
- **Ours:** we show it, and Krum gets to be a person while we do.
  - **He is the only other person in that castle who knows what Harry's life is.** Famous at seventeen, stared at, written about, assumed arrogant, and in fact only interested in being good at the thing. That is the whole basis of it, and it is why his interest is not in the Boy Who Lived but in whether the boy can actually do it.
  - **The Snitch, at the weighing of the wands.** Their first conversation. Harry asks why he ended a match Bulgaria was losing (DIV-066). *"Captain told me to."* A pause. *"And I agreed."* He will not let Harry think he was a puppet, and he will not take the credit off the veteran who called it — it was a decision by a man who knew the game, and Viktor was humble enough to listen. It also tells Harry, who has no team, no captain, and is not speaking to Ron, something he cannot use.
  - **He joins the Duelling Club** with the other visiting students (DIV-028), and keeps coming because he is curious.
  - **He watches the Durmstrang challenger taken apart, and approves** (DIV-029). The student had been running his mouth about both of them, and Krum is glad to see someone put him in his place. The challenger is already settled as not being Krum, so this costs nothing and Krum and Hermione stay exactly as in canon.
  - **Quidditch does the rest.** Two Seekers with no season to play ([quidditch](../threads/quidditch.md)), and Madam Hooch's lesson (DIV-091).
  - **He is the only professional in the maze.** Real contests, real crowds, real stakes, since he was fifteen; everyone else is a student playing at it. It is why he knows the shape of a contest — and why he loses, because Harry does not fight in one. He is formidable, seventh-year and Durmstrang-trained, and he is beaten by a fourteen-year-old, which only works because Harry is the genuine article (Q-123).
  - **He is the first person Harry has to put down,** and the only one in the maze who understood him.
  - **The Snitch answer is characterisation, not a plant.** It needs no payoff elsewhere, because it has already had one: the World Cup beat pays as the argument between Harry and Dracaena about it (DIV-066). *Rejected by the author:* mirroring the World Cup in the maze, since a duel has no margin to salvage and nothing is takeable inside that loss short of going kamikaze; and relocating the mirror to the graveyard, which was not worth the reach.
- **Why:** canon spends Krum and gives him nothing back. Ours buys the DA's premise for free — in B5 nobody needs persuading that Harry can teach, because several hundred people watched him beat the Durmstrang champion (Q-125) — and it gives Harry a friendship that has nothing to do with the scar, in the book where he is most alone. He gets no arc of his own and this is not a rival-suitor thread.

### DIV-091 · B4: Madam Hooch's flying lesson

- **Status:** settled. Open: the cast and what happens in the air (Q-126).
- **Canon:** nothing. Quidditch is cancelled for the Tournament, so the one thing Harry is unarguably good at is absent from the book, and Krum's flying is never seen outside the World Cup.
- **Ours:** Hooch still teaches the first years to fly. A few months into the year, with a world-class player in the castle, she calls a special session and invites the schools' Seekers, who would all want to see the young star up close. It is an exhibition, not a contest: they show off for the children.
  - **Nobody duels.** Harry against Krum is kept for the maze and must not be spent early. Both outcomes are bad in their own right, too: Harry losing damages him in front of Dracaena, and Harry winning makes a fourteen-year-old better than a professional at the professional's own game (the author's call).
  - **Nobody wins.** The session produces exactly one result — Krum does the thing he is famous for, and we find out who follows him down. Harry does. She does.
  - **The cast.** Dracaena, Cho and Cedric are all on the lawn with Harry and Krum. Fleur watches from the stands and is miffed at being left out of the champions' gatherings — she is a social butterfly from the start, and this is where that reads. The stands are full, because Krum has a fan club.
  - **The hour before the show is domestic.** Harry and Dracaena go among the first years correcting their technique and bickering about how it should be done, in front of everybody, and the watching school decides they are a married couple. The children adore it. She is *nice* to the small Gryffindors, because she is enjoying herself too much to remember not to be.
  - **Then the show, and the Feint.** Krum does the thing he is famous for and they all follow him down. Cedric and Cho pull out earliest; then Dracaena; then Harry, a moment after her; then Krum, last, because it is his. **Harry loses and it costs nothing:** a dive is not a duel, the star is allowed to take this one home, and Harry against Krum stays unspent for the maze.
  - **He is not looking at Krum.** All the way down his eyes are on her, and he pulls out the instant *she* bails, because she is who he is flying with and he is having the time of his life. He has entirely forgotten what they were supposedly doing. So has she: she is looking at him and nobody else. **Which is why his second place means nothing** — he was never in the contest.
  - **What it is really for.** It assembles the maze's cast months early, enjoying themselves — three of the four champions, plus her, plus the other house Seekers, in the air, with children watching. In June three of those people are in a hedge trying to put each other down, and this is what the reader has to remember while it happens.
  - **Two things it picks up for nothing.** It is a first years' flying lesson with Hooch, which is the scene that started Harry and started the two of them: same teacher, same lawn, three years on, and the text never points at it. And B4 has no Quidditch while B5 takes flying off him for life (DIV-067), so this is close to the last time he flies for the pleasure of it, and the book does not know that.
- **Why:** the only flying in the book; Krum made a person rather than a poster; and the maze's cast shown liking each other before the Task sets them on each other.

### DIV-092 · B4: Harry decides he is a calamity

- **Status:** settled. Open: what the Diggorys themselves say (Q-124).
- **Canon:** the Diggorys visit the hospital wing and thank Harry for bringing Cedric's body back; Harry pushes the sack of gold at Mrs Diggory on the grounds that Cedric got there first, and she refuses it. His guilt afterwards is diffuse survivor's guilt that nobody ever addresses squarely, and the gold goes to the twins as a throwaway.
- **Ours:** he holds himself responsible, in a way he never has before, and the book says so.
  - **What is new is failure.** Every other time, something tried to kill him and he came out on top. This time he did not manage it, and it cost somebody else his life. From that he concludes that he is a **calamity** — a misfortune that happens to the people near him.
  - **The premise is false and nobody corrects it.** He settles on *if I had not been in it, Cedric would have won.* On the evidence of the maze that is almost certainly wrong: the real final was Harry and Krum, and Krum would most likely have taken the Cup (DIV-089). Nobody puts this to him, because nobody is thinking about the bracket.
  - **The gold is cut from the scene.** It was going to be him making amends with the only thing he had, and it does not belong: the visit is for Cedric's parents, not for Harry's conscience. Where the money goes instead is DIV-062, and the idea is born in a different conversation entirely.
  - **The visit is Harry giving them closure,** and it does not need to be gentle. Cedric knew exactly what he was entering, and the book is not going to apologise for the fact that Harry beat him first. **Q-124** is what they actually say, given that both of them watched Cedric lose on the relay.
  - **The hair, just before they come in.** She combs her own in a hurry — she has spent the night in her day clothes in a chair against his bed and it shows — then gives him a pitying look, has a go at his, and gives up once she works out that his hair simply does not take it. **The same conclusion Molly Weasley reaches a year later,** trying to make him look respectable for his Ministry hearing (canon). Two women who love him, the same hair, the same defeat, and nothing in the text joins them.
  - **Dracaena is in the room,** because she refused to leave — she held the whole castle off his bedside the night before and has not moved since ([the-hospital-wing](../scenes/b4/the-hospital-wing.md), DIV-058). Her upbringing is good for exactly one thing here and it is the right thing: she is an expert navigator of a delicate social occasion, and she runs it so that Harry does not have to.
  - **Then, when they have gone, he says he should have pulled out of the Tournament.** If all he brings the person standing next to him is death, he should never have competed. **Which is enormous, and he means it:** retiring meant being stripped of his magic entirely and banished from the wizarding world (DIV-087), so what he is saying is that he should have given up everything he has. It is also, without his noticing, a charge laid at her door, since she is the one who told him it was all right to want to win.
  - **She cuts him off, and will not tell him he did nothing wrong.** She does not argue with the guilt, does not produce comfort, does not take the charge, and does not let him off. What she says is: ***"I'm glad it was you who made it out alive."*** Not reassurance. Not consolation. A flat statement that it was good that he lived. Again.
  - **What she does not say,** and what has to come through anyway: that she would trade a hundred Cedric Diggorys for him. Saying it outright is too much; the intent is not optional. **Harry is her happiness.** He is not to be ashamed of having survived, and nobody gives up and dies because things have got hard.
  - **Which is the only thing that reaches him,** because it is the thing he has never been given and cannot ask for: not absolution, not gratitude, not admiration — being **wanted**, by someone, as himself ([harry-potter](../characters/harry-potter.md), [themes](../core/themes.md)). His found family gives him belonging; she is the one who gives him this.
  - **She is not being kind.** She is being honest, she is hard-hearted, and she is in love, all three at once, and the line only works because all three are true. No softening her for the occasion.
  - **And she does not take her own advice.** Put in the same position in B6 — Marked, used, and certain she is beyond saving — she gives up and sets about dying, which is the whole of the hollow weeks, the breakup and Sectumsempra (DIV-015, DIV-043, [sleeping-beauty](../threads/sleeping-beauty.md)). She does not come back to what she said here until the raid on the Manor in B7 (DIV-049). **The line is hers, aimed at him, and it is the thing she will need and refuse.**
- **Why:** canon never lets Harry articulate the guilt and never lets anybody answer it, so B5's rage arrives half-motivated. Ours gives it a shape — *I am a misfortune* — that runs straight into B5 and B6 and is answered, once, by the one person who would never say "it wasn't your fault". It also keeps the thesis honest: belonging comes from the Weasleys, and being wanted comes from her.

### DIV-093 · B4: who actually makes the badges

- **Status:** settled. Open: who the Slytherin is (Q-129).
- **Canon:** Draco Malfoy makes the SUPPORT CEDRIC DIGGORY badges that flip to POTTER STINKS, and hands them round the school himself.
- **Ours:** he does not, because she is a Malfoy and a Malfoy is a nobleman, not a craftsman. Nobody in that family has made anything with their hands in three hundred years.
  - **The Hufflepuffs make them.** They are the industrious House and Cedric is their champion, so of course they turn out badges for him. Theirs say nothing about Harry: it is a SUPPORT CEDRIC DIGGORY badge and it means exactly that.
  - **The insult is a Slytherin contribution,** added after the fact by an older student — sixth or seventh year, one of the people who made Nola Laurel's life a misery for being Muggle-born (DIV-082), and **delighted to have a new target.** Hufflepuff loyalty supplies the badge; Slytherin cruelty supplies the other side of it. Who he is: **Q-129.**
  - **Not the obvious candidates, and each for its own reason.** Not Daphne Greengrass: she is the next queen bee once Dracaena stops bothering to humiliate Harry, she is a schemer rather than a ringleader of merchandise, and she is already carrying the candidacy for Rita's source (Q-038). Not Pansy: she is Dracaena's real friend (DIV-055). Not Crabbe and Goyle, who are trolls and could not have had the idea.
  - **Dracaena does not wear one.** Not a stance and not a kindness: she has no reason left to disbelieve him about the Goblet, he is already outstanding in her eyes, and joining in with somebody else's insult simply does not occur to her. By B4 the only opinion in the world she fears is his.
  - **Pansy notices.** She is the friend, and a friend notices a badge that is not there. It is a candidate first thread in her working out that there is something between the two of them, well before anyone else does.
- **Why:** canon's version asks us to believe an aristocrat mass-produced novelty merchandise. Ours gives the cruelty a named owner inside the House's actual ugliness rather than treating it as the House's weather, keeps Hufflepuff loyal instead of nasty, and turns the badge into a detector: who wears one, who does not, and who notices which.

### DIV-094 · B3: Slytherin starts to notice

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco's hostility to Harry is uncomplicated, and nobody in his House ever has cause to wonder about it.
- **Ours:** from B3 the arithmetic stops working in public, and two people act on it long before the badges.
  - **The scarf arrives in B3 and never leaves.** She wins it at the last duel of their second year (DIV-059), parades it at home over the summer, and then **uses** it all through B3 — a Slytherin in Gryffindor colours, in front of the entire House, most visibly on a winter Hogsmeade weekend. "Trophy" covers it for a while and then stops covering it.
  - **B3 piles on.** She trains herself into the ground — not to beat him, but to beat him *properly*, which is a distinction nobody else in that House would make (DIV-017, DIV-069). She goes on needling him throughout. The two things do not add up, and people can see them not adding up.
  - **The needling never stops, which is exactly what makes it such good cover.** It runs through B4 right up to the Ball and continues for the rest of their lives; what changes is the undertone, and the surface never changes at all.
  - **Daphne Greengrass makes her move in B3, not B4.** The queen bee is visibly distracted, which is a vacancy, and Daphne is the sort who notices a vacancy before it has finished opening. It also gives her candidacy as Rita's source a year's head start (Q-038, DIV-082).
  - **Pansy starts noticing in B3 too,** and she is the one with the most data and the least appetite for using it, being the real friend (DIV-055). The missing badge in B4 is therefore **not** the first thread — it is the one that finally will not go away (DIV-093).
- **Why:** the secret cannot be kept by authorial convenience in a House of forty people who share a common room. Somebody has to notice, and it is better for us that the two who do are the friend and the rival — one of whom will protect her and one of whom will sell her. It also gives Daphne's rise and Pansy's eventual loyalty a start date instead of an appearance in B5, and it gives the hoard a public face years before Harry finds the private one (DIV-059, DIV-060).

### DIV-095 · B4: the ferret incident is re-aimed at Hermione

- **Status:** settled, including the injury and the hospital wing. Open: Lucius's answer (Q-117, shelved by the author).
- **Canon:** Draco hexes Harry in the back as he walks away, and Moody transfigures him into a ferret and bounces him down the corridor until McGonagall stops it.
- **Ours:** the beat survives whole — the Transfiguration, the bouncing, McGonagall — and the target changes. **Dracaena goes for Hermione Granger, in the open.**
  - **Because the canon version is no longer available.** She spent the back half of B3 proving to herself that she can own a mistake and put it right in order to live up to his example (🔒 DIV-003, DIV-017). She will not curse Harry Potter in the back, in September, three months after she flew a condemned hippogriff to a wanted man for him.
  - **And because she has not become nice.** Hermione is the rival in her head (DIV-041), Hermione is not him, and Hermione is Muggle-born and therefore barely a person to her yet (DIV-074). The scene exists to prove the turn in B3 was about *him* and about nobody else, which is the whole of her cipher ([dracaena-malfoy](../characters/dracaena-malfoy.md)).
  - **What sets it off is a true sentence** *(settled)*: Hermione tells her she is never going to take Harry, because she is a nasty and vile person. **Dracaena hears *take* as *take from me*** — a claim about character received as a claim of possession — and since the only opinion that can reach her is Harry's, and the girl who is always beside him has just claimed to speak for it, she is not insulted but threatened.
  - **It is an assault, not a prank** *(settled, 2026-11-18)*. Crouch does not bounce her, he beats her half to death with the floor: she comes back to human form battered, bleeding and spitting blood, and goes into shock. **Played at canon's register this is slapstick**, and we have spent DIV-086 calling it a Death Eater venting hatred on a child. It should read as one.
    - **Pomfrey mends it inside the hour** — broken ribs are an afternoon's work in this world — so nothing moves in the calendar and there is no convalescence. **The cost is not medical.**
    - **And it makes Crouch's appetite legible.** DIV-086 hangs his destruction on the one thing he has never governed; this is the reader watching it slip, in September, with nothing at stake.
  - **McGonagall draws on him** *(settled)*. Not canon's reprimand — **she has her wand on an Auror and demands he put the girl back**, in front of the corridor.
  - **And because Harry carries her off, Moody cannot march her to Snape as he does in canon.** **McGonagall takes *him* to Snape instead**, offstage, kept as a thing that happened and never dramatised. **He never does anything remotely like it again, so it looks exactly like an intervention that worked** — which it was not, and could not have been, and nobody finds that out for nine months.
  - **Harry laughs, and then he does not.** He laughs at the Transfiguration — she makes a very fine ferret — and the bouncing kills it instantly, because *that is her.* He does not examine the reaction; the reader is ahead of him (DIV-037). **Ron thinks it is the best thing he has ever seen**, which is canon's reaction kept and turned into a measuring stick for how far Harry has already drifted.
  - **Then he picks her up and carries her to the hospital wing.** Unprompted, on impulse, with the corridor watching, and with blood on him. It is the same reflex as going back to her on the pitch at the end of B3 (DIV-069) — the person over the prize, before he has any idea that is what he is doing — and it is the most public thing he does all year outside the Tasks.
  - **She will not let go of him** *(settled)*. In the hospital wing she clutches him and cannot be made to stop — Pomfrey cannot treat a patient who is holding on to a third year, and settles for Harry holding her hand while she works.
    - **It is not romance and must never be staged as any.** It is a girl who has just discovered she is not exempt from being hurt by another person. She has duelled, and she has been slapped (DIV-069); she has never been assaulted. **So she latches on to the nearest thing she reads as competence and protection**, which is the boy who held off a horde of Dementors for her and killed a basilisk at twelve.
    - **And it is her ledger working, which is why it is in character.** She is not being emotional, she is valuing — and under shock she values correctly for the first time in her life. The one Slytherin reflex she cannot switch off is the one that saves her.
    - **It concedes nothing.** She would deny it afterwards and is humiliated by it, and it costs her nothing because she refuses to count it. **Nothing mends in September** (below).
    - **It also sets the balcony up exactly.** In September she grabs him because she cannot help it; at Christmas she asks whether she is *allowed* to. **The balcony is her requesting permission to do on purpose what shock already made her do.**
    - **And it is the first time reality touches her body.** The second is the Mark (DIV-015). Dreams were kinder than the facts both times, and both times she had no say — see [dracaena-malfoy](../characters/dracaena-malfoy.md).
  - **And then he tells her, in the hospital wing, that what she did was hideous.** Not an argument about blood prejudice — he has not got the words and would not try. He is bewildered and disappointed, having just watched the girl who flew a condemned hippogriff to a wanted man for him be vile to his friend. **It is the one channel that reaches her and he finds it by accident**, because her objections run through taste and *ugly* is the only verdict she can act on (DIV-070).
    - **He is angrier about the contradiction than about the cruelty** *(settled)*, which is what keeps him from prosecuting a girl who has just been beaten. Not *you hurt my friend* but *then which one of you is real* — and she has no answer, because she does not know either. **That is the charge she carries to Christmas.**
    - **And it comes after Pomfrey has finished**, when she is sitting up with her face back on — not while she is holding his hands. The armour gets no chance to work, which is crueller in effect and far kinder in conduct.
    - **She does not get a pass for being frightened.** What she did was serious whatever was done to her, and the scene must not trade one for the other.
  - **It is a plant, not a turn, and the ordering is load-bearing.** She promises nothing and concedes nothing. She stops saying the word in front of him, quietly and without admitting to it, and otherwise carries the charge unanswered for three months, because the ledger she runs on has no entry for amends: conceding she was wrong with nothing coming back is not a transaction she has a form for ([dracaena-malfoy](../characters/dracaena-malfoy.md)). **The promise comes after the Ball** (DIV-074, DIV-015, Q-135). Anything that looks like her mending in September steals the balcony's question.
  - **She cannot make the carry mean anything.** Her account is that Potter is decent to everybody and it costs him nothing, so it is not about her; she cannot believe he would want someone like her near him. The conversation is small, awkward and unresolved.
    - **The corridor therefore leaves two debts, and they are paid in different places.** The verdict is answered at the balcony (below). **The carry is answered after the Second Task** (DIV-104), when she finally says out loud that she thinks it meant nothing — and the fact that corrects her is one she has never had: he was furious with her when he picked her up.
  - **Harry does not file it against Moody, and there are two reasons he can get away with that** *(settled)*. **He is angry at her as well**, and the cheapest thing a fourteen-year-old can do with two incompatible feelings is stop looking at them. **And no adult in the castle treats it as a scandal** — Ron thought it was the best thing he had ever seen, McGonagall stopped it, and the man kept his job. Children calibrate off adults. By the time it could matter, it has set as *that is how Moody does things*, and the mentorship is built on top of it (DIV-102).
  - **The Ball pays it off.** On the balcony at Christmas she asks whether she is allowed to dream — *someone like me* — and calls him Harry for the first time. She is asking about this corridor: he saw exactly what she is, and carried her anyway ([yule-ball](../scenes/b4/yule-ball.md)).
  - **Crouch's act is worse than canon's and otherwise unchanged** (DIV-086). Harry files the cruelty under *that is how Moody does things*, likes the man anyway, and carries it — and the confession turns it over: the one act of the year Harry had to excuse for him is the only one that was not his.
- **Why:** canon's beat needs a sneak attack our Dracaena will not make, and a victim whose humiliation Harry is supposed to enjoy. Re-aiming it keeps the clue, keeps her ugly at exactly the point where the romance is most at risk of going soft, and buys the year's largest public gesture and the Ball's central question for nothing. It also puts the first grain of the Ron-and-Hermione rift in September instead of having it arrive in June.

### DIV-096 · B4: Harry is given permission to be a soldier, and gets past the sphinx by attacking it

- **Status:** settled, including that what moves is the threshold rather than the reflex. Open: the rest of the maze's furniture (Q-118). The doctrine itself, and why Moody picks him, is DIV-105.
- **Canon:** Harry solves the sphinx's riddle. Moody's year is generic Defence teaching, and the Half-Blood Prince's spell in B6 arrives out of nowhere, a boy reading an unknown word off a page in a temper.
- **Ours:** what the year leaves in Harry is not a syllabus. It is permission.
  - **There is no list of Dark spells, and that is the design.** Our magic already measures this: Dark magic runs on intent, and Lustrum counts spells rather than names (DIV-084, [magic](../setting/magic.md)). So what Harry absorbs is the doctrine — *spells are weapons, they are not good or bad* — and the stain comes from casting ordinary spells wanting to do harm. There is nothing illicit to find, nothing to fetch out of the Restricted Section, and nothing for Ron or Hermione to object to. They practise in an empty classroom, as in canon, and no one in the room could tell the difference. **The fire could.**
  - **Moody gives permission, not the idea.** Harry has improvised past every problem he has ever had and has never once had the correct answer to anything. The year does not teach him to fight dirty; it removes the apology. **He is told he would make a good Auror** (canon, and sincerely meant), by the most decorated Auror in Britain, in the year he is being hunted — and a fourteen-year-old wears that like a borrowed jacket. It is never his life's ambition. It is a thing that fits him in year four.
  - **What the permission moves is the threshold, not the reflex.** He was always fast, and he has improvised past every problem he has ever had; what he has never had is an adult telling him that going first is correct. Once he has that, it takes less and less to make him act. **A disposition, not a capability** — which is why nothing he does afterwards can be pointed at and called a thing Moody taught him, and why he never becomes a different duellist, only a quicker-triggered one (DIV-105).
  - **The chain to the Conjunctivitis Curse is canon's own, and it is four links long.** Harry does not watch Krum's First Task — he is in the tent — he is *told* about it afterwards, and then **Sirius says it is what he would have done himself.** Precedent, then proof it beats a dragon, then the blessing of the one adult whose approval he actually wants, and only then the decision to learn it. Nobody had to teach him anything; the Tournament and his godfather did the work, which is far worse than Moody handing it to him.
  - **So he blinds the sphinx.** The riddle is hard, Hermione is not there, the maze herds and there is no going back (Q-118), and he is on a clock in front of the entire school. He is running on one thread — *get past the thing* — and the cheapest answer is a spell. **He does not kill her and does not beat her**: the curse buys him seconds and he takes them, and goes through with a sphinx behind him.
  - **The charge makes it work; it does not make him brave.** The decision is dry and his own. What the Goblet's charge does is land the spell harder than he meant, which frightens him (DIV-087). *A power-up must never cause a choice* — the same rule that kept it out of the Yule Ball.
  - **And this is what Sectumsempra is bought with.** Not *he was taught Dark magic*: **he was taught that there is no such thing as an illegitimate move.** Two years of it, hardened by a year of running the DA, where fighting becomes reflex rather than decision, and by having been under the Cruciatus himself. In the bathroom he is not choosing a spell — he is a boy who thinks *if she gets the Cruciatus off I am finished*, and reaches, and the page says FOR ENEMIES (DIV-032, DIV-043).
- **Why:** it makes Harry's dirtiest moments continuous with his best trait instead of being a lapse, it gives B6 a two-year run-up instead of a margin note, and it costs no new mechanics at all. It also keeps Harry's win his own: nothing he does in the maze was put in him by Crouch, only permitted.

### DIV-097 · B4: the flask, and the two drinks

- **Status:** settled, both scenes. Open: how much of the November debrief is on the page.
- **Canon:** Moody's hip flask holds Polyjuice Potion, and the hourly nip is the clue the reader is meant to catch on a reread. Nobody else ever drinks from it.
- **Ours:** it is whisky, and he gives Harry a drink out of it twice — after the First Task, and after the Second. **The two scenes are one motif: a lesson, and then the lesson passed.**
  - **It is the only way to dispose of the flask.** Ours is innocent (DIV-086), but a reader who knows the original is watching it regardless, and Moody drinking from it proves nothing — the twist is that it *looks* like nothing. **A second person drinking it and staying himself is the only version that settles it on the page**, and the alarm it buys first is the biggest free beat in the book: for about a line, every reader of canon is certain Harry Potter has just drunk Polyjuice Potion.
  - **And it is an honest signpost.** Having spent the loudest tell the original has, the book has told the rereader where not to look: the props are clean this year, and what gives the man away is the man (Q-114).
  - **The trunk is in the room, and nothing is made of it.** The thing to suspect is twelve feet away, asleep. The joke pays twice, and only the second time hurts.
  - **It plants the word the real tell breaks, and the word is *check*.** Not *pour* — the rule he teaches is trust but verify, stated as a drill aimed at Harry in the warmest scene the two of them have. So the Butterbeer at Hogsmeade is not a man drinking something he did not make; **it is a man taking a stranger's drink without doing the thing he personally taught the boy to do** (Q-114).
  - **He treats Harry as a colleague back from a job, and means it.** The scene is a debrief, not congratulation: what he did right, what he did late, what would have killed him against something that could think. **He is the first adult all year to treat the dragon as an engagement Harry survived** rather than an ordeal Harry was put through, and the whisky is the end of that sentence, not a treat. His frame is the Auror office and not a school, which is why the boy's age never arises.
  - **The shout is the scene.** Harry drinks, nothing happens to him, and *CONSTANT VIGILANCE* nearly puts him off the chair — then the dressing-down: dead twice over, he could have been handed poison just then, **he should have checked it, rookie mistake.** Harry's defence is sound and still not enough (*you drank it too, sir*; an antidote beforehand is a classic trick), and it closes on the blessing — *do that, and you'll go far*. **It also discharges the Polyjuice scare as a laugh** instead of letting it fizzle: the reader braced for the wrong reason and got startled anyway.
  - **No mischief in it.** No *don't tell Dumbledore* and no shared crime. If he offers it as a transgression it is a cheap gag; he offers it as procedure, and the comedy is the gap between his frame and a castle's. The gesture is warm, sincere, entirely his own, and indefensible, all at once.
  - **It is DIV-096 in a single image.** What the year leaves in Harry is permission rather than a syllabus, and the whisky is the social half of it: adult standing he has not earned and should not want, handed over by the most decorated Auror in Britain, in the year he is being hunted. Nobody else in the castle offers him that, which is why he loves the man.
  - **The offer is sincere and the test is real, and they are the same act.** Not a trick dressed as a gift and not a gift spoiled by a trick: full marks would have been Harry casting to detect, finding nothing, drinking, and Moody approving. **He cannot hand the boy anything without turning it into a drill, and he means both halves.** His own vigilance has an off-switch marked *among allies* and he knows it — the man sniffs a chicken leg at Grimmauld and eats it, with half a nose — so what he is really demonstrating is that the rule has exceptions.
  - **And the first exception is him.** What Harry takes out of the room is *you are safe with me*, which is true, and which is not true for the five hours of the year when Crouch is riding him — and Harry will never be able to sort which was which. **It is the real man's doing and not the thing in the trunk's**, which is far worse than hanging the breach on Crouch: Moody is complicit in his own instrument-hood without committing a single bad act. On a reread it is the bitterest thing in the book, and he does it smiling, in November.
  - **February: he throws it, and Harry passes.** Coming up from the lake with Dracaena, on the way to celebrate, Moody calls him over and tosses him the flask — *for a job well done!* **Harry grins, draws his wand, casts a detection charm he looked up in his own time, finds nothing, and drinks** — without coughing, much — then throws it back.
    - **So the full-marks version is not a fantasy any more.** November planted it; February pays it, which turns the first scene from a gag into a setup and shows the year accumulating in the boy rather than being described.
    - **The verdict is a compliment wearing a criticism:** *you were only dead once this time, Potter, you're improving. Next time, eyes on the target instead of the misdirection — oldest trick in the book.*
    - **And it is the year's reading instruction, said out loud and missed.** Harry checked the flask and stopped watching the man who handed it to him. **The flask is the misdirection and Moody is the target**, which is true tactically, true of the book, and true of what happens in June. Three times now the flask has told the reader where to look.
    - **It is short.** They are on their way to a party and Dracaena is waiting; nothing else happens in it, and Moody says nothing whatever about her — **he said all of that in December** (DIV-101). **She holds on to Harry harder all the way through it**, which he does not understand and she will explain within the week (DIV-104).
    - **It also makes Grimmauld land.** One swig in a lifetime is sad; a habit between the two of them is something Molly Weasley can be furious about (DIV-098).
  - **It is the real Moody throughout.** No take-over, no plan value, nothing Crouch wants from it. Another thing Harry will never be able to sort out afterwards, except that this one he can: it was his.
- **Why:** canon's flask is the one clue our premise makes useless, and leaving it lying innocent in the background wastes it. Spending it deliberately buys a scare, a signpost and the setup for the tell that replaces it, and gets the year's clearest picture of what Moody actually gives Harry for the price of half a page.

### DIV-098 · B5: the prefect party, the Firewhisky and the Order photograph

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** at the celebration for Ron and Hermione's badges, Moody — a man who has known Harry a few days — produces a photograph of the original Order and hands him his dead parents, naming the murdered as he goes. Mrs Weasley is later found losing to a Boggart that wears her family's corpses, and Lupin destroys it.
- **Ours:** both halves turn, because ours taught the boy for a year.
  - **Sirius tries to slip Harry a Firewhisky**, in the spirit of the evening and partly to see what happens — **and Moody butts in fondly**: *gave you a taste for that stuff, eh, Potter?* (DIV-097). There were two of them, which is a habit rather than a lapse, and he says so.
  - **If it needs to go off harder, he calls him *junior Auror*** — his form of address from B4 (DIV-102) — in front of the boy's mother in all but name. It is the axis stated in two words and Molly hears it as what it is.
  - **He is not confessing.** He is reminiscing, with no notion that he has handed Molly Weasley an indictment, because in his own accounting that night was one of the better things he did all year. Sirius is delighted and makes it worse; Molly goes off like a boiler.
  - **The laugh has a floor under it.** She has landed on the one memory he is using as proof he did right by the boy, and he will defend it to the last precisely because it is the part he is sure of. The reader, who knows what else he did that year, watches him pick the smallest possible hill (DIV-100).
  - **And it is the axis in public for the first time:** two men and a drink on one side, a mother on the other (DIV-099).
  - **The photograph is aimed at the room, not at Harry.** Ours would not put James and Lily into the boy's hands as an opener — he has debriefed him, drilled him and poured him whisky, and he knows exactly what he is holding. **He gets it out because he is in a nostalgic mood**, and works through the old Order one by one, and *his* nostalgia means saying precisely how each of them was taken apart.
  - **Harry is the one who leaves.** He gets as far as his parents and goes, and Moody is still going when he does. **This is not tact and not tenderness**: it is an old soldier enjoying himself in a room full of people who loved the same dead, and it is the same tin ear that bounced a schoolgirl down a corridor and thought it just.
  - **Canon's chain is otherwise untouched.** Harry finds Molly alone with the Boggart, and Lupin dispatches it. *The echo is free: Moody is naming how his friends died in one room while she is looking at her children's bodies in the next.*
- **Why:** canon's photograph beat depends on Moody barely knowing Harry, which our B4 spends a year destroying. Re-aiming it keeps every piece of the sequence, keeps the man tactless rather than making him kind, and lets the whisky come back once — in front of the one person guaranteed to object — without a scene being built to hold it (Q-138).

### DIV-099 · B5: the Sirius–Moody axis

- **Status:** settled, including that he works it out (Q-140, answered). Open: how much of the Order's argument is on the page.
- **Canon:** the Order's argument about how much to tell Harry runs roughly as a reckless godfather against a protective mother, with Lupin mediating. Moody has no particular stake in it.
- **Ours:** Moody is on Sirius's side of it, for his own reasons, and the two of them together are the loudest voice in the house.
  - **It follows from B4 and cannot be avoided.** A man who ran a debrief, taught a doctrine and poured the boy a drink for a job well done cannot turn round six months later and argue that he is a child who should be told nothing (DIV-096, DIV-097).
  - **His reason is a defence mechanism, and the reader should be able to see it.** *I made a soldier of him, and it is the one thing I did right last year.* Everything else he did in 1994–95 ended at a headstone; this is the single piece he can hold up, so he holds it up hard.
  - **He has the service record to argue from, and it is genuinely frightening:** a dragon, a lake, a maze stocked to kill, three champions carrying a Tournament's charge, and then Voldemort — plus a castle stormed by Dementors two years before that (DIV-081). Put on a desk, it is a better record than most of his former colleagues ever had.
  - **It raises the argument out of temperament.** Molly is no longer up against one reckless man but two professionals with the boy's file open, which makes her harder to dismiss and much more desperate, not less sympathetic. **She is also right.**
  - **And it loses.** Harry is kept in the dark regardless, because that is B5's engine. What the axis buys is that his fury gets a target: competent adults said tell him and were overruled, so he is a man being managed rather than a boy sulking.
  - **Lupin is the voice of reason** and does not resolve it. He can see that both sides are right and says the minimum, which is what he is for (Q-136).
  - **Neither of them is actually looking at Harry, and that is the point.** Sirius sees James; Moody sees a soldier. Both are failures of sight dressed as faith, which is how Harry stays the loneliest person in a house full of people who agree with him.
  - **The bill comes due at the Ministry, and he is standing in it.** Canon puts Moody in the rescue party, where Dolohov drops him in one move. **Ours is a terror there** — it is where the promise the reader has been carrying since Dumbledore's office gets paid — and it does not matter in the slightest. He fights beside the child soldier he built, and Sirius dies anyway, a few feet away. **He is not told about the Ministry afterwards; he was in the room.**
  - **And he gets it.** *I called a teenager a fellow veteran to keep my own ego intact, and it has killed his godfather.* He made a child soldier and told himself it was a fighting chance, and the correction arrives too late to be worth anything. **He does not keep his cool about it** (DIV-100, DIV-103).
  - **It is the second of four rungs, and he goes down all of them.** The prefect party with his ego intact (DIV-098); the apology, where it first cracks (DIV-100); the Ministry, where he watches it; and the Seven Potters, where he applies the correction exactly once and it kills him (DIV-103). **He keeps making the same mistake and it keeps costing somebody else.**
- **Why:** it makes the Order's split structural instead of temperamental, it gives Moody's guilt a shape that is self-serving rather than penitent, it keeps Harry isolated while surrounding him with allies, and it hands B5's catastrophe a cause that was planted in B4 rather than one that arrives with the plot.

### DIV-100 · B5: Moody apologises for the ferret

- **Status:** settled, including where he says it. Open: when Harry passes it on (Q-139).
- **Canon:** the ferret was Crouch's act in Moody's body, and the real Moody never speaks of it to anyone.
- **Ours:** ours did it — remembers doing it, and remembers having thought it just (DIV-086, DIV-095) — and some time in B5, once he has had the months to work out what the year actually was, he says so to Harry.
  - **What he says is *I failed you both*.** Not the Imperius Curse and not self-flagellation about having enjoyed it. **His shame is professional, not moral:** he was taken at his own house before term began, and he should have fought it off, and a schoolgirl was bounced down a corridor because he did not.
  - **It is airtight, because he did throw Crouch off in the end.** Dumbledore's office is proof that it could be done, which turns every month he did not do it into a failure he can measure. His one heroic act of the year is the evidence that indicts him.
  - **It is also a veiled apology for the whole year** — the Goblet, the Portkey, the graveyard, Cedric Diggory — delivered sideways, about a corridor, because that is the piece small enough to say out loud.
  - **Harry hands him the excuse and he will not take it.** *It wasn't you, you were under the Imperius* — which is, word for word, the defence Lucius Malfoy walked free on, offered to him by the Malfoy girl's boy, and he declines it (Q-117).
  - **It is a sentence, not a speech.** He admits defeat and leaves. If it runs longer he turns pitiable, and he must not; **he is not asking to be forgiven and does not wait to find out whether he is.**
  - **And he asks Harry to pass it on to her**, because he is not going to seek out a Death Eater's daughter and because he is a coward about exactly one thing. **Whether Harry delivers it, and when, is Q-139.**
  - **Where it happens** *(settled, 2026-11-18)*: **the night they bring him out of Privet Drive.**
    - **They settle back into the old routine on the way** — he is the one adult who treats the boy exactly as he did a year ago, and the year has not made him gentler.
    - **And the security question proves nothing, which is the joke and the horror.** Harry asks something only the real man could answer, and gets it right, **and it is worthless: nobody ever impersonated Alastor Moody.** He was himself all year, under a curse no one in Britain can test for (DIV-086). One of them should say so. *A lighter alternative, if the scene wants warming rather than chilling: Harry hands him water and apologises for not having checked it for curses.*
    - **Then the flight, Grimmauld, and Harry is sent upstairs to Ron and Hermione** (canon). **Moody catches him by the shoulder on the way.** The apology, the relay request, and nothing else. He does not follow him up.
    - **And he is downstairs vouching for him inside the hour**, beside Sirius. **The two things in the same evening are the whole character:** he is not torn. He thinks *I failed you* and *I made you good* are both true, and he is right about both (DIV-099).
    - **It therefore comes before the prefect party**, which makes the party a man who has already cracked once clinging harder to the one thing he is still sure of (DIV-098).
  - **It does not soften the axis** — it is the same instinct. In his own accounting he failed the boy by not treating him as a combatant soon enough, which is precisely the wrong lesson, and the reader can see that and Harry cannot (DIV-099).
- **Why:** the apology canon never has to write is the one our Moody owes, and the two obvious versions are both unusable — pleading the Imperius makes him Lucius, and relishing the memory makes him a monster he isn't. Taking responsibility for the defeat rather than the act is the only version in character, and it quietly indicts the thing he is proudest of.

### DIV-101 · Both men give Harry the same verdict on Dracaena

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** no adult assesses Harry's romantic life, and nobody has an opinion about a Malfoy.
- **Ours:** the two men who end up on the same side about everything else arrive at the same verdict about her, independently, in different registers — and neither of them disapproves, which isolates them from every other adult in the books.
  - **Moody assesses.** *Malfoy's spawn, eh. She'll either be the death of you or your greatest asset* — threat assessment, not morality, offered the way he would brief anyone, and framed as a warning about **cavorting with the enemy**.
  - **Placement: days after the Yule Ball**, in the conversation that also carries the Winky tell. **He needs no trigger, because he was there.** He spent the evening watching Harry Potter dance with Lucius Malfoy's daughter and admired the socks himself; nothing in a gossip column could tell him anything he had not already seen. **The few days are deliberate** — he sits on what he watched, decides it is a problem, and then moves on it, which is how an Auror handles anything. He would not wait until February.
  - **It is the first link of a four-link chain, and the chain is the point** *(Q-114)*: **cavorting with the enemy → the warning → and why is the Malfoy elf giving you socks? → Dobby is minding another elf, Winky → Moody startles.** The question about Dobby stops being a convenient opening and becomes the next sentence in an argument already about the Malfoys.
    - **And Harry is arguing about his girlfriend while it happens**, which is a far better reason to miss the startle than being oblivious.
  - **At the Ball itself, nothing but the socks.** Moody admires them in passing, as in canon; **Dracaena stiffens against Harry's arm and lets go of it when he walks away.** Harry feels the grip change, does not understand it, and loses the moment before he can ask — the first and only time the corridor surfaces between the Ball and the lake (DIV-095, DIV-104).
  - **He calls him *junior Auror* while he does it** (DIV-102), which is the funniest and worst possible framing for a warning about a girl.
  - **And the warning is the compromised man in the room** telling the boy to watch the wrong family.
  - **Sirius blesses.** *With Slytherin women you either hit the jackpot or an early grave — either way, it's worth it.* **He is talking about his own family**: the Blacks are Slytherin women, he means his mother and his cousins, and he is telling Harry to go for it anyway. **It goes where it already belongs** — it is how he plants the Yule Ball (DIV-019).
  - **The difference between the two lines is the axis in miniature.** Moody says *or* and means a warning; Sirius says *either way* and means a blessing. **Harry hears the same sentence twice and takes the second one**, which is the shape of everything that happens to him in B5 (DIV-099).
  - **Moody's half is wrong in the one way that counts.** The Malfoy to be frightened of is the father, and the father is the one who opens Harry a way out of the graveyard (DIV-057).
  - **And both halves come true.** She is the death of him and his greatest asset: she is the reason he is on a headstone and the reason he walks off the field in B7 with her wand in his hand (🔒 DIV-006).
- **Why:** it gives the axis a root a year before B5 needs it, it hands DIV-019 the dialogue it has been missing, and it puts the series' thesis about her into the mouths of two men who are each half right and neither of whom is looking at her at all.

### DIV-102 · B4: Moody builds Harry across the year

- **Status:** settled (the rungs and what goes on each). Open: how much of rung three is on the page.
- **Canon:** the year's Defence teaching is generic, delivered by an impostor, and there is no relationship to track — everything the reader likes about it belongs to somebody wearing a face.
- **Ours:** it is a relationship, and it escalates. **Four beats, spread across the year, each one raising what he is willing to hand over** — because spending it all in one scene wastes it, and because B5's axis has to be built on something the reader watched happen (DIV-099).
  - **Rung one, November: the drink after the First Task.** The debrief, the shout, the word *check* — and *you are safe with me* (DIV-097).
  - **Rung two, February: the flask thrown back at the lake.** Harry checks it, passes, and is told he was only dead once this time (DIV-097).
  - **Rung three, spring: the Conjunctivitis Curse, and the doctrine.** **Moody walks in on the empty-classroom practice** — Ron and Hermione brace for a bollocking that never comes — **and approves.** That is where the line goes: *spells are weapons, Potter. Hit them first, hit them hard, and you won't be an easy target when you go down.* **Not *if*** — the gallows humour is how he talks, and the fatalism is the part Harry absorbs without noticing.
    - **He is not making the sphinx his decision.** Harry has already decided, off his own bat, by way of Krum and Sirius (DIV-096). **What Moody supplies is the stamp:** the thing you were going to do anyway was the right call. That is permission in its purest form and it is the whole of what the year gives him.
    - **Which is why it cannot sit next to the maze.** Said in the spring it is climate; said the week before the Third Task it becomes an instruction, and the sphinx stops being Harry's.
  - **Rung four, before the maze: nothing.** A professional's nod and no doctrine at all. Whatever Harry does in there is his.
  - **And the stay-behind about the Malfoy girl** sits between rungs one and two — days after the Ball, in the conversation that carries the Winky tell (DIV-101, Q-114).
  - **He calls him *junior Auror*,** and means it as an address rather than a joke. It is the axis stated in two words, a year before the axis exists — and it is the single most incendiary thing he could say in front of Molly Weasley (DIV-098, DIV-099).
- **Why:** the year has to be seen accumulating in the boy instead of being described, and the reader has to like the man doing it. It also keeps DIV-096 honest: four beats of permission and not one lesson in fighting dirty, so nothing Harry does in the maze was put in him — only allowed.

### DIV-103 · B7: Moody's last stand

- **Status:** settled (the fight, the promise and the eye). Open: the choreography, and what the Elder Wand hunt runs on now (Q-011, Q-029).
- **Canon:** Mundungus panics and Disapparates, leaving Moody alone; Voldemort kills him offstage, and the reader is told about it afterwards. The body is never recovered. His eye ends up screwed into Umbridge's office door, and Harry takes it back and buries it in a wood.
- **Ours:** the same deaths, fought for — and the one time the reader sees what he can actually do.
  - **The promise is made at the briefing, flat.** *I'll get you to the destination, Potter, if it's the last thing I do.* Said to the room, as the brief, because he is on a different broom and does not do sentiment. **It only becomes a vow in retrospect**, and he keeps it.
  - **Mundungus bolts and he takes it in stride.** He brought Dung and knew precisely what he was; he took him because he needed bodies. **The man whose whole doctrine is *never take what you haven't checked* is abandoned by the one man in the formation he did not bother to check** — and he was not deceived, he made a trade.
  - **Then he turns and fights.** Voldemort comes for his pair on canon's own logic — Potter will be riding with the best fighter they have — and finds the best fighter they have. **He kills Death Eaters.** The reader has been owed this since Dumbledore's office, where he broke a hold nobody else in Britain could break, and this is where the debt is paid (DIV-086).
  - **When Harry's Expelliarmus gives him away**, a Death Eater calls it, and **Moody makes Voldemort deal with him instead.** Voldemort cannot turn his back on Mad-Eye Moody throwing curses at him, and that window is how Harry and Hagrid get out. **Harry is still identified**, which is what the rest of the book needs.
  - **Voldemort kills him**, and he has done exactly what he said he would at the briefing.
  - **It is the correction, applied once.** He spent a year telling Harry he was a soldier and spent B5 defending it; **he dies getting the boy carried out like a boy** (DIV-099).
  - **It replaces canon's wand event as the exit.** The holly wand acting on its own is already unsettled (Q-029) and the allegiance chain it fed is already gone (DIV-024), so Moody's stand costs the Elder Wand plot nothing it still had. **What the hunt runs on instead is logged at Q-011**, with the author's standing lean: Voldemort wanted the thing already.
  - **The eye is carried, not used.** Still not recovered after the Ministry; Harry takes it off Umbridge's door as in canon and **keeps it until he can bury it properly.** No artifact, no seeing through walls, no rules to write — a see-through-anything object would answer Gringotts, the snatchers and the Manor before they were asked.
    - **The Order recovers nothing of him**, so the eye is all there is to bury. **Deliberately not a motif** *(author, 2026-11-18)*: it stays a fact of the scene and is never echoed or called back, and it is not tracked on Harry's file.
  - **The vigil at the Burrow lands differently.** Canon's grief is for a hard old man who died badly. Ours is for the man who made Harry what he is, did real harm doing it, and then spent his life keeping a promise — all three at once, with no way to sort them.
- **Why:** canon's Moody dies offhand because canon's Moody is a cautionary tale with one book of real presence. Ours has an arc, an unpaid promise and a debt to the boy, and all three resolve in the same ninety seconds. It also gives the Seven Potters a reason for Harry to survive it that does not depend on wand lore we have already rebuilt.

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** nothing. Draco is never hurt by Moody in a way that needs answering, and there is no relationship in which it could be raised.
- **Ours:** the corridor's second debt comes due a few days after the lake, and the subject is not really Moody.
  - **Nothing is said about it between September and February, and the silence is active.** Up to the Ball they have no reason to raise it — he is not at the centre of either of their minds while Harry is working himself up to ask her. **Afterwards she is holding her breath.** One night does not undo four years, she does not entirely believe him yet, and she will not go near a dangerous subject in case it costs her the thing she has just been given ([sleeping-beauty](../threads/sleeping-beauty.md), DIV-013). The only surface it breaks is the flinch at the Ball (DIV-101).
  - **The Goblet is what changes it, and it works on her for a precise reason.** Her standing defence since September is *Potter is decent to everybody and it costs him nothing* (DIV-095) — and an impartial instrument with no interest in flattering anybody looked at Harry Potter and named her (🔒 DIV-005). **She cannot run that sentence past a referee.** She can refuse a kindness, a gift and an amends, because her ledger has no form for any of them; **a verdict from a thing that gains nothing by lying is the one kind of evidence she can accept** (DIV-087, [dracaena-malfoy](../characters/dracaena-malfoy.md)).
  - **So she raises it, and she quotes the flask.** Not the corridor — *you laughed with him, and I was holding on to you while you did it* (DIV-097).
  - **And the question she asks is his own, handed back.** *Which one is real?* — the exact charge he laid on her in the hospital wing in September, which she never answered and has been carrying ever since.
  - **Harry has no tidy position and must not be given one.** In February he does not know Moody is innocent and never will until June. **What he says is that it was monstrous, that he cannot give the man up, and that he does not understand that either.** He does not defend Moody and he does not promise anything about him.
  - **The answer is the corridor, and it is a fact she has never had.** She has assumed the carry was reflexive decency, which is precisely why it is worth nothing to her. **He was furious with her when he picked her up** — he had just watched her be vile to his friend, he was angrier at her than he had ever been in his life, and he did it anyway and did not think about it. **He is not comparing her to Moody; he is telling her what he is like, and she is the evidence.** She works the rest out herself, and a conclusion she reaches is one she cannot argue with.
    - **He never cites the Goblet.** The Goblet gives her the nerve to ask; his answer has to predate it by five months, and it does.
    - **The physical beat is September reversed:** in the hospital wing she would not let go of him; here **he is the one who does not let go of her hands.** No speech required.
    - **And he must never say they are alike.** Out loud it is an insult and it is smug. The comparison is hers to make.
  - **She does not thank him and does not concede.** She goes quiet, or deflects, because that is what she does with anything she cannot repay. **What she leaves with is that she is not disposable to him.**.
  - **Moody is left exactly where he was** — she does not forgive him, Harry does not disown him, and the thread goes quiet until the person who actually owes her an apology makes one (DIV-100). **It does not drag; it is waiting for its proper owner.**
  - **And June annuls both of their positions.** Harry learns the man he refused to give up never did it. She learns she spent a year hating someone innocent of the only charge she had against him. **What survives is what they said to each other in February, which was true regardless of whose hand it was** — and the apology that finally arrives in B5 is for a different failure than either of them ever imagined (DIV-086, DIV-100).
- **Why:** the ferret cannot simply sit there once it hospitalises her, and neither of the obvious outs works — Harry giving Moody up guts the year, and her accepting Moody makes her a doormat. Routing the scene through the carry instead of through Moody resolves the only thing that can be resolved, pays off a plant laid in September, uses the locked Second Task for something beyond romance, and leaves the Moody debt standing for the man who owes it.

### DIV-105 · B4: Moody's doctrine, and why it is Harry

- **Status:** settled (the doctrine, the succession, and the three reasons). Open: how much of the casualty record is ever said out loud.
- **Canon:** Moody's teaching is a catchphrase and a set of temperamental tics, delivered by an impostor. *CONSTANT VIGILANCE* is characterisation rather than a position, nobody ever asks what the man thinks Defence is actually for, and his interest in Harry is Crouch's cover story.
- **Ours:** he has a doctrine, he is right about it, and he has chosen an heir.
  - **The doctrine is aggression, and it is a professional conclusion rather than a temperament.** Counter-curses are well and good, and by the time you need one you are already losing: a step behind, reacting, and depending on the other man to have made a mistake. **So you go first and you go hard.** The enemy will not be kind and will not wait for you to finish your shield.
  - **His body is the evidence, and he teaches from it.** The leg, the nose, the eye — every one of them bought defending, by a younger man who thought that was the job. He changed policy afterwards and **he buried fewer of his own.** That is a casualty record rather than a feeling, and it is what turns *CONSTANT VIGILANCE* into a syllabus: always ready, always prepared, always first. **Showing a room of fourteen-year-olds his injuries as a teaching aid is entirely in character and entirely indefensible.**
  - **The story must never refute him on the merits.** He is right, and the book does not get to pretend otherwise. The cost is not that the doctrine is wrong; it is that he is handing it to a child, and a man who has reduced himself to a function cannot see why that would matter (DIV-096, DIV-008).
  - **He is finite, and he has done the arithmetic.** Not spent — he is as dangerous as he has ever been, and the reader sees it three times (DIV-086, DIV-099, DIV-103) — but old, wounded, not improving, and one man. He is going to die in this war. **So the plan is succession**, which is what the four rungs of the year actually are: a handover, not a mentorship (DIV-102).
  - **His whole self-valuation is the count.** What he is worth is how many Dark wizards he has taken off the board, and the only way that number goes on rising after he is gone is through somebody else. It is the only form of continuation available to a man who has nothing else. **It is also the ledger that the one good thing he does for Harry never appears on.**
  - **Why it is Harry — three reasons, mundane on purpose.** No prophecy and no Chosen One: he does not need one and is never given one.
    - **The debt.** He put the boy's name in the Goblet and remembers having had a reason at the time (DIV-086). He cannot explain it, he cannot undo it, and he keeps nearly saying it and being stopped — so he does the one thing left to him and makes the boy survivable. **The teaching is restitution**, and neither Harry nor the reader is ever told so.
    - **The dead.** He sees the Potters in him, and he is an inventory of people he outlived. Same register as the Order photograph a year later, which is why that scene reads as continuous with this one (DIV-098).
      - **And there are two of them in the castle.** Frank and Alice Longbottom were Aurors and were in the Order, and Moody knows exactly what was done to them, so Neville is on the same list. **He is not a candidate in 1994** — the boy who melts cauldrons is four years short of the one who holds the line (DIV-025) — and Moody is not a man who picks on potential. **So Neville gets the kindness and Harry gets the handover**: canon's gentleness after the Unforgivables lesson is the real man's and is entirely sincere, and it is as far as it goes. Moody dies without seeing what Neville turns into.
    - **The evidence.** The boy throws off the Imperius in his classroom, in front of him, when nobody else in the room can — and then walks off a dragon in one piece and well. **The First Task is the trigger**, which is why the handover starts that evening and not in September (DIV-097, DIV-102). The rest is file: three years of it, plus a castle stormed by Dementors (DIV-081).
    - **And he is receptive**, which is the saddest part of the arrangement. Nobody has ever treated him as a colleague, so when somebody finally does he takes it with both hands.
  - **Casting the Imperius on students is the doctrine, not a mystery.** You cannot learn to beat a thing you have never felt, so he puts it on them — **which is exactly what this man would do whether or not he were under one himself**, and no further explanation is needed or offered. The irony belongs to the reader; it is not evidence, and it is not a trail he is leaving (Q-114).
  - **But the doctrine has a limit, and it is utility rather than ethics.** He would never put the Cruciatus on a student. Not because it is Dark — he does not grade spells that way (DIV-096) — but because it cannot be resisted and cannot be trained against, so there is nothing in it but hurting a child. **He demonstrates all three on spiders and casts only one of them on people**, and the line he draws is drawn with a professional's reasoning, which is worse than squeamishness would have been.
  - **The bitter part stands regardless.** The thing that decides him on Harry is the boy throwing off the curse that is riding Moody as he watches.
- **Why:** canon's Moody is a set of tics with an impostor inside them, and ours cannot be, because the whole of DIV-096 is Harry absorbing what the man believes. Giving the doctrine a casualty record makes it correct, which is the only way the damage it does is interesting rather than cautionary. And succession explains the shape of the year (DIV-102), the B5 fixation (DIV-099) and the last stand (DIV-103) out of a single motive instead of three.

### DIV-106 · B4: Moody resigns, and the last drink

- **Status:** settled throughout, including that Harry is told why (Q-141, answered).
- **Canon:** the Defence post is jinxed and nobody holds it two years running. Moody never taught at all — the man in the castle was Crouch — so the real Moody has no relationship to the job, no intention about it, and no exit worth the name.
- **Ours:** **he means to stay, and then he takes himself out of the castle.**
  - **He meant to stay.** Not a year's favour to an old friend: he has found the form his succession can take (DIV-105), and a classroom is a hundred children a year for as long as he lasts. He says so during the year, and the reader should have it well before June.
  - **Then he resigns, and nobody makes him.** A Death Eater lived inside him in this castle for ten months; he taught a hundred children *constant vigilance* with a hand on his throat; a champion is dead. **His shame is professional, and it is the same shame as the apology** (DIV-100) — he was taken at his own house before term and should have fought it off, **and he knows it could be done, because he did it in the end.** A man carrying that cannot stand in front of that class again, and does not try.
  - **Dumbledore asks him to stay and he refuses.** For the first time in years the castle has a real Defence teacher, and loses him to his own conscience. **The jinx's instrument is the man himself**, and the Ministry walks into a vacancy nobody had to engineer.
  - **It is what makes B5 inevitable.** He surrenders the sane version of passing the torch — a hundred children, every year — and keeps the insane one: the single boy. *I made a soldier of him, and it is the one thing I did right last year* is the position of a man who has just given up everything else he had. **The fixation is what is left when the classroom goes** (DIV-099).
  - **He is perfectly well, and is never in the hospital wing.** Canon's Moody comes out of the trunk half-starved; ours walked the castle all year and nothing was ever done to his body. **There is no convalescence**, so he is on his feet and clearing a classroom within days of the Third Task (DIV-086).
  - **The farewell, in the last week of term. Harry finds him packing**, in the Defence classroom rather than the office — the dark detectors coming off the walls, the room being handed back. *(November's scene was the office and must not be reused.)*
    - **He tells him he is going, and why.** No evasion and no softening: a Death Eater lived inside him in this castle for ten months, he taught a hundred children *constant vigilance* with a hand on his throat, and a boy is dead.
    - **Harry does not protest, because Moody is right and he knows it.** That is the scene's whole shape, and it is what keeps it from being Lupin's goodbye a year on.
    - **And he does not offer the Imperius excuse here**, although he will in August (DIV-100). **He has not had time.** It is days after the graveyard; he has had no month alone to turn it over, and has not yet watched the man do the Order's work. In June he is a boy agreeing with an adult who sounds certain. By the night they bring him out of Privet Drive he has built a position, and it turns out to be Lucius Malfoy's. *(Where he builds it is not settled. Her summer letters are the obvious candidate, given whose defence it is — DIV-065.)*
    - **Then the flask.** *I never gave you a prize for winning the tournament.* Harry checks it without being told and without taking his eyes off him, casts, drinks, and hands it straight back.
    - **"You made it this time, Potter. Full marks."** **And Harry hears the subtext**, which is why the scene exists: *I have nothing left to teach you.* It is not praise. It is a discharge, and it is the loneliest thing anybody says to him all year.
    - **Harry keeps nothing.** The flask leaves with Moody and is never recovered, like everything else of his (DIV-103), so **the eye stays the only object of his that anybody ever carries** — and the instruction that it is a fact and not a motif holds.
    - **It closes the object's year:** poured for him in November, thrown to him in February, offered outright in June (DIV-097, DIV-102).
    - **It is not a fifth rung.** The rungs stop before the maze by design and nothing is handed over here (DIV-102); the scene only marks that the handing over is finished.
- **Why:** the post has to change hands for B5 and canon's jinx will do it for nothing, so the only real question is whose hand is on the knife. Making it his own costs no machinery, follows directly from the shame already settled in DIV-100, and turns DIV-099's doubling-down from a character flaw into the only thing he has left. It also buys the farewell, which pays off the year's warmest motif.
  - **The Lupin problem, and how it is answered.** B3 already ends with Harry finding a Defence teacher packing. The resemblance is deliberate from here on and the differences carry it: **Lupin is forced out and Moody goes of his own accord**; **Harry argues with Lupin and does not argue with Moody**, because this time the man is right; **Lupin leaves him something to keep and Moody leaves him nothing**, since the flask goes straight back. Lupin's goodbye costs Harry a link to his parents; Moody's tells him he is finished, which is not a loss and is worse. **And Harry notices the pattern** — two years, two Defence teachers, and he is about to get a third.

### DIV-107 · The Killing Curse is a projection

- **Status:** settled (the mechanism); the numbers and what is legible from outside are Q-143
- **Canon:** Avada Kedavra is a jet of green light that travels like a projectile, cannot be blocked by any shield, kills whatever it touches, and requires meaning it. Canon never addresses why a spell with no counter and no visible cost is not simply the opening move of every fight.
- **Ours:** **it is not a spell the caster throws. It is the caster, thrown.** Avada Kedavra projects the caster's entire magic out of the body, which then has to come back, and every other property follows from that.
  - **Nothing stops it, because it is not scaled like a spell.** Shields are built against castings; this is a whole person arriving at once. It goes through Protego and through any ward ever devised, and it does not need to strike anywhere in particular — it envelops rather than hits.
  - **No travel time inside its reach, and nothing whatever outside it.** Wand, word, green light, and the man drops. Past its range the projection cannot hold itself together and dissipates entirely: no falloff, no wounding at the edge, nothing arrives at all. **Duelling range, not battlefield range** (Q-143).
  - **It needs an unobstructed path through matter, not an unobstructed view.** Seeing is targeting; matter is the wall. A pane of conjured glass stops it dead and still lets the caster watch you through it, which is the entire basis of DIV-108. **The pinnacle of the Dark Arts goes through every shield ever devised and is stopped by a plank**, and that is the correct shape for it: alien, not weak.
  - **It is a wave with a small field, not a point.** It pushes forward and spreads a little, which is why it only has to reach you *somewhere* and why it has never cared about clothing. **So it does not see thin material at all** — cloth, paper, a leaf. **Stopping it takes mass**, and some materials take the load better than others. **Nobody survives this by wrapping up in a blanket**, and the defence is therefore a Transfiguration problem rather than a prop (DIV-108).
  - **So the enemy has to close.** That is the staging of every confrontation with Voldemort in seven books — a man walking towards you through the spells being thrown at him, because the only way to use the best thing he has is to be near. He cannot snipe, and he never could.
  - **The caster is empty while it is out.** No magic to cast with and none to defend with, for as long as the projection takes to go and return, and longer the further it travels. Casting it means voluntarily being nothing for a moment, and almost everyone experiences that as a small death. **That, and not conscience, is why nobody spams it.**
  - **Which makes it an ambush weapon or a group weapon, never a solo one.** Somebody has to hold the line while you are hollow. It is one of the reasons Voldemort keeps followers at all — and the one time he went to do it himself, alone, it ruined him.
  - **He is the fastest and surest caster of it alive, and the aptitude is the damage.** A man already in pieces is long used to not being whole, so the hollow moment does not frighten him as it frightens everybody else. Inside his reach, anyone who drops their guard is finished. **His being the best at it and his losing are the same fact.**
  - **There is no clean casting, ever, by anyone.** Dark magic runs on intent (DIV-084) and this spell has no second use, so there is no other way to cast it: the caster launders their entire being through malice and not all of it comes back. **One casting marks a person permanently** — the heaviest single residue there is, and what makes any Death Eater worth the name a pyre under Lustrum (DIV-080).
  - **And it cannot be cast in a flinch.** The draw is the longest and least forgiving radical string in the Dark Arts (DIV-007), so it is a decision held visibly for seconds in front of whoever is watching. **Nobody has ever killed with it by accident.**
- **Why:** canon's Killing Curse is a worse pistol with better branding, and nothing in canon explains why the war is not over in an afternoon. One mechanism now answers the range, the cost, the rarity, the unblockability, the mass it takes to stop and the moral weight at once.

### DIV-108 · Interposition: putting matter in the way

- **Status:** settled; the staging is Q-144 and Moody's mobility teaching is Q-149
- **Canon:** defence is Protego, dodging and counter-curses. Terrain is scenery, and in seven books of duelling almost nobody puts a solid object between themselves and a spell on purpose.
- **Ours:** **you move, and you use cover.** Matter answers what shields cannot, the Killing Curse included (DIV-107) — and it is an advanced technique, not a reflex anyone can reach for.
  - **It takes real Transfiguration, at speed, under fire, in the right place.** **Mass is the requirement.** The curse is a wave with a small field rather than a dot, so it goes through cloth, paper or a leaf without noticing and needs heft to stop; some materials take the load better than others, and knowing which is part of the skill.
  - **So the ceiling is Transfiguration**, which is where this earns its keep past one lesson. It is what finally makes Harry apply himself to the subject, and what puts something concrete behind McGonagall's advice in B6: keep studying with me if you mean to be an Auror, because they use it.

  **How Moody gets there.** He watches Flitwick's Duelling Club and does not recognise it as fighting — tame, decorous, conducted standing still. So he takes the theory into his own classroom.

  - **It is posture before it is technique.** *You do not stand in the open and you do not stand still.* Move, and keep something between you and the other man.
  - **Shields and counters leave you on the back foot**, and a counter-curse requires you to already know the answer. **A block works every time, including against the things that have no answer.**
  - **He concedes that both have their place, which is what keeps him credible.** Bouncing something back is sometimes the right move; countering keeps your own offence up. **What he objects to is the instinct** — meeting a Dark wizard head-on, matching him spell for spell, against a repertoire you have never seen, when being wrong once is permanent.
  - **He points at a wound to make the point.** *This is what I got for trying to counter the —.* The body is the syllabus again (DIV-105), and this time it is an argument rather than a warning.
  - **The demonstration is asked for, not volunteered.** Somebody wants to know what a man who cannot dodge is supposed to do about any of it. **So he shows them:** he floats a desk, turns it to stone in the air, and puts it where the spell was going. The leg is the reason the technique exists and he makes no speech about it. Transparent cover is the refinement — he will not give up seeing what the other man is doing — and whether it belongs in this lesson or later is Q-144.
  - **And a student finds the hole in it.** Fire that snakes around cover, and it does. The answers are to trap it with geometry, which is a very high ceiling and which he demonstrates, or to move and accept being singed. **Not Harry**, who takes to the technique rather than arguing with it; who raises it is Q-144.
  - **Mobility is the other half and it is not designed** (Q-149). He cannot simply be a walking fortress, and flashy magical movement would demote brooms and turn duels into spectacle, when these are meant to be battles of wits.

  **What it does for people other than Harry.**

  - **Hermione can do this.** It is pure technique and costs almost no power — the first fighting skill in the series she is actually built for. She is still not a fighter, and Dracaena, Harry and Ron all outclass her in a duel, but she now has something to drill that makes her useful *in* a fight rather than only before one.
  - **And it pairs her with Ron.** **He attacks and she covers him.** He is a pure-blood and has the power ([society](../setting/society.md)); what he lacks is finesse, and she is nothing but finesse — so each of them is doing the half the other cannot. **It does not work with Harry**, who is too unruly and chaotic to be covered: she would only get in his way. The first thing in the series the two of them are mechanically good at together.
  - **Harry has a field day with it.** It is the missing piece of his style: he already improvises, and this hands him the environment (DIV-028).
  - **And he invents his own variant: bouncing his own spells off placed Protegos.** Nobody taught him this and it is entirely his. **It wins him exactly one bout against her, and she hates it more than any defeat in the series** — it looks like missing on purpose, and being beaten by it is being beaten by a clown. **That is her taste firing, not her pride** (DIV-070): it is not that he was better, it is that it was *undignified*. Where it lands is Q-144.
  - **It gives the DA a spine in B5**, with Balthier Thynne the obvious partner: a seventh-year Slytherin who is genuinely good at Transfiguration and has joined (DIV-082). Nola watching Balty turn out to be the useful one in the room is free, and warm.
  - **He teaches it to the whole class, and it is his biggest contribution.** **Not a fifth rung, and DIV-102 is untouched**: the four private handovers stand and nothing precedes the maze. This is the classroom version of the succession (DIV-106), so the thing of his that outlives him is the one he gave away to everybody — and it appears on his own ledger nowhere (DIV-105).
  - **Harry finishes Krum with it** (Q-123): the eye that reads the draw is the Duelling Club's, the ground is Moody's.
  - **And it kills him.** In the air over Surrey there is no mass to raise and nothing to get behind (DIV-103).
- **Why:** Moody's year has to leave Harry something he can hold, or DIV-105's succession is rhetoric. It gives DIV-107 a counter a person can actually perform, so the Killing Curse is terrifying without being an "I win" button; it makes Transfiguration matter for the first time in the series; and it hands the Battle of Hogwarts a grammar.

### DIV-109 · Dracaena's wand-tip shields

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Draco has no signature technique. He is competent and nothing about his magic is characterised.
- **Ours:** **she shields on the point of her wand.** Not a wall in front of her body — a Protego the size of a coin, made at the wand tip and put exactly where the incoming spell is going to be, so that she pricks curses out of the air and turns them aside. It is the cheapest possible way to block anything, and it is only available to someone with absolute control over the shape and placement of her own magic.
  - **It is the whole character in one piece of technique.** She spends nothing and wastes nothing; she simply puts the right thing in the right place at the right instant. It is the duelling form of the same precision that makes her the best potioneer in the castle (DIV-011) and the better technician of the two (DIV-028).
  - **It conserves her,** which matters in a long fight and matters more against anyone stronger. She can bounce things back off it, and does, which is how she gloats.
  - **And it cannot touch the Killing Curse.** Nothing scaled like a shield can (DIV-107). **The one incoming thing in the world that her art has no answer to is the only one that matters**, and she knows it perfectly well.
  - **There is nothing to try.** You cannot practise against it and there is no counter to research, so her awareness of it is not a project she failed at — it is simply something she knows, and knows painfully.
  - **Her objection to interposition is not taste, it is concession.** Using cover means admitting the other duellist is better. **Moody grants the point himself** (DIV-108): if you know the answer, a counter or a shield is the stronger move, because you can afford not to defend and keep the other man answering you instead. **That is exactly where she lives** — a pin-shield costs her nothing, and her knowledge of curses and their counters is wide enough that she usually does know the answer. Interposition is for people who do not.
  - **So she scoffs, and then it is taken off her.** Harry tries it on her and she overcomes it, which is what makes him perfect it — and the mature version is what beats Krum (Q-123). **He does it so cleanly that she has to concede it is worth knowing.** She still prefers offence and always will.
  - **Where she concedes is a decision** (Q-144). *The clean version:* she grants the **technique** on her own in B4, on the evidence, and still refuses to credit the **man** — and it is Moody's apology, relayed in B5 (DIV-100, Q-139), that finally gets her to say his name without spitting. That splits the two concessions, keeps the apology scene about the apology, and lets her look past her own wounded pride under her own power.
  - **And it probably does not happen in the Duelling Club.** Flitwick's club is a noble art with rules, and he can think the technique correct and still not want it dragged over his piste — which gives Harry a reason to work on it elsewhere and makes the bout where she overcomes him a private one. Open: Q-144.
  - **It is her ceiling again** (DIV-070): she loses the B3 final to an improvisation she finds distasteful, she loses in B5 to something her code does not price, and the one unanswerable curse in the Dark Arts is the thing her technique cannot reach.
  - **So B7 is not a better shield.** She cannot block it, so she answers it instead — Expelliarmus, her wand, his hand over hers (🔒 DIV-006). **The thing that beats the Killing Curse is not a defence. It is two people.**
- **Why:** it makes "she is the better technician" something the reader watches rather than something the bible asserts, it gives her a named fear with a mechanical shape, and it sets the two schools of defence against each other along the line their characters already run on. It also turns the locked ending into the answer to a problem the reader has watched be unsolvable for four books.

### DIV-115 · Lindsay Madison, and the shop that can't say no

- **Status:** draft
- **Canon:** Madam Malkin fits Harry while Draco stands on the next stool. She has an assistant in later books who never speaks. There are no American witches anywhere in the series.
- **Ours:** **Lindsay Madison**, an American witch in her twenties, is Madam Malkin's clerk, and the fourth person in the room at the first meeting.
  - **She is the witness.** Harry has no idea what has just happened to him. She does, from the first cold question, and she can't stop it — all she can do is step between them, get him off the stool, and tell him afterwards that some folks get awful passionate about the subject of a person's birth. Her *tch* is the reader's permission to be angry on his behalf while he is still working it out.
  - **She knows exactly who he is, and keeps her voice down about it.** Her eyes go to the scar in the first minute. What she does is wink, ruffle his hair and say she gets stared at all the time, and that is the last anyone in the shop hears of it. **Everyone at the Leaky Cauldron was kind to him** — kind *because* he was Harry Potter, which is the thing he is already sick of. She is the first adult who is kind to him without making it about that, and the restraint is the kindness.
  - **The fringe stays down.** She does not get him to stop hiding it and does not try, which is also why the girl by the window never works out who she is talking to (DIV-009).
  - **Look.** Tall, pitch-black hair knotted up with oversized dressmaker's pins, a close-cut sable robe of crow feathers — a walking crow, and frightening to an eleven-year-old until she opens her mouth. Frightening again on the way out.
  - **Voice.** A Southern drawl carried in word choice and rhythm, never in phonetic spelling: *git*, *sugar*, *darlin'*, *little bitty*, *I declare*, *carry him on over*, *some folks get awful…*. Eye-dialect is out — it reads as mockery and ages badly.
  - **The shop's economics,** which is the real reason she earns a ledger entry. After the Malfoys go she asks why they don't simply refuse them, and Madam Malkin shows her the blank cheque: *they don't even write the price themselves*. The old families keep the Alley afloat and school robes don't. **The pure-blood case stated from the losing side, in book one, with nobody arguing it** (DIV-008).
  - **She is imported labour,** which is quiet evidence for a Britain that is shrinking (DIV-035). Never spelled out.
  - **B4:** Narcissa sends her to Hogsmeade with the Yule Ball dress, and she has forgotten nothing ([the-fitting](../scenes/b4/the-fitting.md), Q-150 answered).
- **Why:** the scene needs an adult who sees it happen, Harry needs one warm face before the world turns cold on him, and four lines of shopkeeper do more for DIV-008 than a page of argument would. And she is far too good to spend once.

### DIV-116 · B4: Harry charms Narcissa at the World Cup

- **Status:** settled
- **Canon:** Harry meets Lucius and Draco in the Top Box. Narcissa is present, says nothing, and nothing whatever passes between her and Harry. She is dour in all her appearances.
- **Ours:** Harry is introduced to Narcissa Malfoy and, with no idea on earth what he is doing, charms her flat. She responds by having a lark at everyone's expense, chiefly her husband's and her daughter's.
  - **The warm-up, from across the stands.** Ron or Hermione points out the Malfoys, and says that must be Narcissa, Malfoy's mother. Harry says *no wonder Dracaena is that pretty, then.* His friends round on him; he has no idea what they mean, because he is certain he only thought it (DIV-117).
  - **The introduction.** Lucius presents his wife to the box. The niceties pass, and then Harry notices she has not stopped looking at him. *You must be Harry Potter, then. My daughter has been regaling me with tales of your accomplishments* — which is true, and is the four years of complaining Lucius has been listening to, delivered back as a compliment.
  - **The queen.** He blurts it out, exactly as he blurted out the other one three years earlier (DIV-009). **Queen, not empress**, because it pairs with princess ([sleeping-beauty](../threads/sleeping-beauty.md)).
  - **And she goes.** Giggling, helplessly, leaning on her husband in front of the Minister for Magic and half the box.
  - **The hand.** Still laughing, she holds it out: *well, now I want a proper greeting.* **He doesn't clock that it wasn't a request** — he can feel he is meant to, so he does it — and that finishes her off completely.
  - **The fallout, all comedy.** Dracaena sulks, because *she* is supposed to be the princess and has never once got anything like it. Lucius is exasperated by his wife's antics, and comes out of the scene well for it: **she is known for teasing him affectionately in public, and he bears it**, which is the warmest thing anybody sees a Malfoy do in four books.
  - **Narcissa is much less dour than canon's**, and the series was already there — this is where it is first shown rather than asserted ([narcissa-malfoy](../characters/narcissa-malfoy.md)).
  - **Under the comedy, it is where the alliance starts.** Narcissa is Harry's ally in all but name for the rest of the series, and this is the reason: not politics, not strategy, nothing to do with the Dark Lord. She met the boy once and liked him (Q-039).
  - **Same hour as the veela** (DIV-037). The boy nothing can touch is undone by nothing at all, and flattens her mother with it, while her daughter watches.
- **Why:** it makes Narcissa's alliance something the reader watched begin instead of something the bible asserts; it shows Harry's charm working on somebody who is not Dracaena, which is what stops it being a romance convenience; and it is funny.

### DIV-117 · Harry says it out loud

- **Status:** draft
- **Canon:** nothing like it.
- **Ours:** **a running gag.** When Harry is absorbed enough — and it is almost always about Dracaena — the thought comes out of his mouth, and he is certain he only thought it. Somebody else always hears.
  - **The first one is the World Cup:** *no wonder Dracaena is that pretty, then* (DIV-116).
  - **He never believes he said it.** Challenged, he asks what they are talking about, and means it. There is no embarrassment, because there is no awareness, which is what makes it funny and what makes it evidence.
  - **It is the same root as the charm** — no self-consciousness at all when he isn't thinking about himself ([harry-potter](../characters/harry-potter.md)). The charm is what it does to her; this is what it does to everyone standing nearby.
  - **It is the only reliable leak in the series.** He cannot say what he wants out loud on purpose and does not know he wants it, so the only evidence that ever escapes him escapes by accident. **Which means the gag is also the plot's instrument**: it is how his friends work him out long before he does ([harry-dracaena](../threads/harry-dracaena.md), [reveals](reveals.md)).
  - **Ration it.** Once a book at most, and never where a scene is carrying weight. Where it lands after B4: Q-152.
- **Why:** the romance needs his side to be visible to the reader while staying invisible to him, and a gag does that in one line where interiority would need a paragraph.

### DIV-118 · The fake love triangle

- **Status:** draft
- **Canon:** Rita Skeeter invents a romance between Harry and Hermione out of nothing, for copy. Nobody else believes it, and it is a press problem rather than a social fact.
- **Ours:** **everybody believes it, for three years, and Rita is the last one to the story rather than the source of it.** There is no triangle — Hermione has never felt anything of the kind and Harry has never once thought about it — and that is precisely why it works: it is a misreading the whole world arrives at independently, which is what makes Dracaena's version of it reasonable instead of paranoid (DIV-041).
  - **It is always the same evidence, and the evidence is Hermione's hands.** She feeds him, makes him work beside her so she can catch his mistakes, has his broom confiscated for his own good, slaps people on his behalf, and checks him over for injuries. **No ambiguous behaviour is ever involved.** It is one girl's affection, in plain sight, read as a different kind of affection by everyone who sees it.
  - **She never once notices.** Not in three years, not from anybody. It is her tragedy from the other side: being taken for a lover is the one thing her usefulness has never got her ([hermione-granger](../characters/hermione-granger.md), DIV-041).
  - **Who gets it wrong, and when.**

| When | Who | On what |
|---|---|---|
| B2, Flourish & Blotts | **Molly Weasley** | Harry comes out of Knockturn Alley filthy and Hermione throws herself at him and checks him over. Molly already treats Harry as a son, reads it as a girlfriend, and is delighted |
| B3, Diagon Alley | **Lindsay Madison** | Hermione is on his arm in the shop. Lindsay ribs him about not compliment­ing other girls in front of his young lady, and Hermione asks, perfectly sincerely, why everyone keeps saying that (DIV-119) |
| B1–B4 | **The school** | Ambient and unexamined. Canon's B4 does the work unaided: Ron isn't speaking to Harry for weeks after the Goblet, so he and Hermione are visibly alone together all autumn |
| B4, the World Cup | **Dracaena** | The veela leave him cold, so he loves somebody; Hermione is sitting with him. Wrong by one person (DIV-041) |
| B4, November | **Rita Skeeter** | Her first Triwizard piece has Harry finding love at Hogwarts, and names Hermione — **because that is the intel everybody already has.** She is not fabricating; she is printing the consensus |

  - **Rita amplifying rather than inventing is the point.** Canon's Rita can be dismissed as a liar. Ours can't, because she is right about what everyone can see and wrong only about what it means, which makes the article real fuel: it hands the school's assumption back to the school in print, and it hands it to Dracaena in November, a month before the Ball.
  - **What Dracaena does with it.** *Your* **other** *girlfriend* becomes a permanent fixture of her vocabulary — first as fear, then, once the fear is gone, as the scathing way to refer to Hermione Granger when she has given up the slur for Harry's sake. It is already in use within a minute of the fear dissolving ([the-little-basilisk](../scenes/b4/the-little-basilisk.md), DIV-038, DIV-041).
  - **Where it ends:** the Yule Ball, in one night, for everybody at once — Hermione on Viktor Krum's arm and Dracaena on Harry's, with Rita printing the real story the next morning. **Nothing in that depends on Krum's role.** Whatever his part turns out to be and whatever the fallout is, the triangle dies in every head present, simultaneously, and the thing that kills it is simply the two of them walking in on other people's arms.
  - **The joke is never at her expense.** It is at the expense of everyone who gets it wrong — Molly, the school, Rita, Dracaena. Hermione is doing nothing in any of these scenes but being fond of a friend in public; the comedy is entirely in what other people make of that. **If a scene ever lands as Hermione being ridiculous, it is the wrong scene.**
- **Why:** Dracaena's jealousy has to be a reasonable reading of the evidence or it is just spite, and the cheapest way to prove it is reasonable is to have sensible people make the identical mistake. It also converts canon's Rita subplot from a fabrication into an amplification, which is worse for everyone and better for us.

### DIV-119 · B3: Lindsay again, in Diagon Alley

- **Status:** draft
- **Canon:** Harry spends the last fortnight of the holidays at the Leaky Cauldron and does his shopping. Madam Malkin's is a line, if that.
- **Ours:** he forgot robes, so the three of them go, and Lindsay Madison is behind the counter. Short — the shortest of her three appearances, a door opening and closing ([the-robe-shop](../scenes/b3/the-robe-shop.md)).
  - **It is her POV**, and that is the whole technique. She shows the reader her work and tells nobody in the room a thing. From inside Harry's head the scene would have to dodge his feelings constantly; from outside, we simply watch a boy complain for a minute and then bristle when somebody agrees, and watch a dressmaker read it (style: multiple POVs, settled).
  - **Her opening.** *Well, if it ain't the Harry Potter. Regret not giving me that compliment all those years back?*
  - **His answer, and the rule it keeps.** He is not smooth and must never be: he says, plainly and sincerely, that she frightened him half to death — which is true, and is not a compliment. **Lindsay decides to be flattered by it anyway** and makes a show of fainting. The joke is that he still can't do it on purpose (DIV-116, [harry-potter](../characters/harry-potter.md)).
  - **She ribs him blind, and the princess is girlfriend number one.** She warns him off complimenting other girls in front of his young lady — meaning herself, meaning nothing — and makes a show of how fast he gets through them: there was the little blonde princess back when, and now there is this one on his arm. **Two already, at thirteen.** She is working the only piece of ammunition she has, which is that she watched an eleven-year-old get flattened by the eyes line. Hermione, on his arm, asks sincerely why everyone keeps saying that (DIV-118), and **Ron takes hold of the *other* girlfriend** — the blonde one — and wants to know who that is.
  - **Then she asks after the princess, and Harry vents.** Unbearable, better than him at this, did that in March, has his scarf and wears it where he'll see it — an inventory of exceeding precision, delivered by a boy certain he is complaining. **It is the mirror of *my daughter has been regaling me with tales of your accomplishments*** (DIV-116): she can't stop talking about him to her parents, he can't stop talking about her to his friends, and neither hears it.
  - **The tell is not the inventory — it is what he does when Lindsay agrees with him.** She says the girl sounds like a right piece of work, and Harry corrects her instantly. *She's not. She's just—* and stops. **He can complain about her all day and cannot let anybody else do it.** This is **not** the leak (DIV-117) — he knows he is speaking. It is a separate reflex, and its largest showing costs him Quidditch for life in B5 (DIV-067, [harry-potter](../characters/harry-potter.md)).
  - **She has no prior intelligence and does not need any.** She shot in the dark with the only thing she had, hit dead centre, and knows it — **and says nothing about it**, which is restraint again, which is how she is kind (DIV-115). She knows the name without guessing: Narcissa collected the child in front of her in 1991, and the Malfoys have one daughter.
  - **The crow clerk.** Ron and Hermione looked for Harry in the shop a few hours earlier and missed him, and come in still talking about the uncanny clerk in the crow feathers. Harry says *oh, that's Lindsay* — **he has her name, from one hour, two years ago**, and thinks nothing of it. **She overhears all of it from behind the counter**, which is the first thing that happens in her POV and a small gift to open on.
- **Why:** it makes Lindsay a fixture rather than a two-point callback across three books, and it gives her a different job each time — witness in B1, the first person to work it out in B3, gloater in B4. It also plants the fake triangle on an outside witness a year before the World Cup, and gets Harry's defensive reflex on the page at its smallest.
